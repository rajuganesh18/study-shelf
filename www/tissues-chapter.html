Part 1 · Why plants and animals are built differently
01Fixed in place, or free to moveWhy plant and animal tissues differ20 XP

A mango tree and a mango-eating bat both need food, support and transport. One of them cannot walk anywhere to get any of it.

Tap each box to cycle it: blank → ✓ true of that group → ✗ not true of that group.

Most plants are fixed in one place and need support to stay firm and upright, so plant cells have a cell wall that provides rigidity and strength. Without a rigid cell wall, animal cells can change shape easily, and that flexibility eventually helps make their bodies suitable for locomotion.

Their nutrition differs too. Animals have tissues that help them digest food obtained from different sources, while plants have tissues that help them use solar energy to synthesise food components through photosynthesis. Growth patterns differ as well, because the tissues responsible for growth differ in structure and function.

Roots in Jar A continue to grow in length. Roots in Jar B stop growing after the tips are cut. This shows that roots grow only from their tips, and that the tips consist of cells which divide continuously.

Growth like this requires actively dividing cells that together form a meristematic tissue. Plants have growth zones at the tips of their roots and shoots called the apical meristems, which help the plant grow in length. Plants grow in three ways: increase in length, increase in girth, and regrowth after cutting or grazing.

03Three ways a plant growsApical, lateral and intercalary meristem45 XP

A seedling becomes a tall tree. Stems become thicker with time. And grass grows back after the mower has been over it. Switch each one on.

—

The apical meristem, located at the root and shoot tips, increases length. The lateral meristem, arranged in a ring along the circumference of the stem, divides and produces new cells inside and outside in a concentric manner, increasing girth. The intercalary meristem, located at the base of the internode or just above the node, helps grasses and cut hedges regenerate.

The node is the point on a plant stem where branches or leaves arise. The part of stem between two nodes is called the internode. Together these three meristems account for growth in length, girth and branching in plants.

From the chapter
If you have visited a timber yard or seen the cut trunk of a tree, you may have noticed ring-like patterns on the cut surface. These are annual growth rings. Some are wide and some narrow, reflecting favourable or unfavourable growth conditions in a particular year. Counting them lets scientists estimate the age of a tree.
04Built for dividingWhat a meristematic cell looks like25 XP

A cell that divides again and again all day cannot afford to be full of storage space. What would such a cell look like inside?

True or false for a meristematic cell.

The cells of meristematic tissues are small, have thin cell walls, a large and prominent nucleus, and dense cytoplasm with many organelles. Vacuoles are generally absent and the cells are tightly packed with little or no intercellular space. These characteristics allow them continuous and rapid cell division.

05Losing the ability to divideDifferentiation25 XP

New cells keep appearing behind a growing tip. Not all of them go on dividing. Put the stages in order, earliest first.

Due to continuous cell division, meristematic tissue adds new cells to the plant body. Some of the newly formed cells remain meristematic while others lose the ability to divide. Those that lose it undergo changes in structure and function and become permanent tissues, specialised to perform specific functions such as support, transport or storage.

This process, by which meristematic tissue becomes specialised to perform specific functions, is called differentiation.

06The outermost layerEpidermis, cuticle, stomata, root hair30 XP

What protects a plant from mechanical injury, water loss, harmful microorganisms and extreme conditions? Match each part to the job it does.

Tap a part, then tap its job.

The epidermis forms the outermost layer of the plant body. It consists of a tightly packed, single layer of flat and rectangular cells, covered with a waxy layer of cutin called cuticle. In plants living in very dry habitats the epidermis may be covered by a thick layer of cuticle to reduce water loss.

In roots, hair-like projections called root hair increase the surface area for absorption of water and minerals from the soil. In leaves, the epidermis contains pores called stomata, which apart from gaseous exchange help in transpiration — the evaporation of water vapour through stomata. Transpiration helps in water transportation by creating a transpiration pull in xylem, and also helps eliminate wastes.

07Bend, or breakParenchyma · collenchyma · sclerenchyma45 XP

Why does a fresh twig bend in the wind while a dry one snaps? Build the same stem three times, out of three different tissues, and push it over.

—

Parenchyma consists of living cells with thin walls, loosely packed with intercellular spaces. It mainly stores food but also performs photosynthesis in the green parts of the plants. In aquatic plants, specialised parenchyma forms air spaces which help them float.

Collenchyma consists of living cells with unevenly thickened corners due to pectin deposition, a chemical that gives flexibility like rubber. This tissue provides support and flexibility, allowing stems and tendrils to bend without breaking.

Sclerenchyma cells have thick walls due to deposition of lignin, making them hard and strong and forming the woody structure. Most of these cells are dead. This tissue is found in stems, leaf veins, and hard coverings of seeds and nuts such as coconut husk and walnut shell.

09Water up, food downXylem and phloem · complex permanent tissues45 XP

Xylem is the tissue that transports water and minerals from the roots to other parts of the plant, and also provides strength. It consists of tracheids, vessels, xylem parenchyma and xylem fibres. Tracheids and vessels are tubular and thick-walled. Xylem parenchyma are the only living component of xylem.

Unlike the xylem, phloem is mostly made up of living cells. It consists of sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma and phloem fibres. Sieve tubes are long and tubular, joined end to end by perforated walls, and transport food from leaves to other parts of the plant. The cellular functions of sieve tube cells are regulated by companion cells, which are specialised parenchyma cells that monitor loading and unloading of sugars.

Together xylem and phloem are known as complex permanent tissues, because they are made up of different types of cells working together.

10Three tissue systemsDermal, ground and vascular25 XP

In a plant body these tissues do not work alone. They are organised together into larger groups. Sort each one into the group it belongs to.

The dermal tissue system forms the outer covering of the plant, protecting the inner parts and reducing water loss. The ground tissue system forms the main body of a plant between the dermal and conducting tissues, and includes parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma. The vascular tissue system consists of the conducting tissues — xylem and phloem.

Ready to go beyond
In young plants, the outer protective layer is a single-layered epidermis. As plants grow older, some cells below the epidermis of the stem develop the ability to divide, act as lateral meristematic cells and form the cork cambium. Cork cells are dead, compactly arranged, and impermeable to water and gases. This forms the bark of the tree.
Part 3 · Animal tissues
11Five linings, five jobsEpithelial tissues · Table 3.235 XP

Blink your eyes. Take a deep breath. Something is lining the surface in both cases, and its shape follows its job.

Tap a structure, then tap where in the body you would find it doing that job.

Epithelial tissue forms the outer covering of the body — the skin — and also lines internal organs such as the mouth, lungs, blood vessels and intestine. It is composed of closely packed cells with very little space between them. This structure prevents the entry of germs, reduces water loss, and helps in the absorption, secretion and movement of substances.

Different types of epithelial tissues are structurally adapted to perform different functions: exchange, protection, secretion, sensory functions and absorption.

12What holds you togetherConnective tissues · Activity 3.335 XP

Touch your elbow gently. Press and fold your ear. Wiggle your fingers and feel your forearm. Each one tells you about a different tissue.

Tap what you feel, then tap the tissue responsible.

A tissue that connects and supports other tissues is called a connective tissue. Both blood and bones are connective tissues, yet blood is fluid while bone is hard. This difference is due to the matrix, which is watery, soft and jelly-like in blood but hard, solid and rigid in bones.

Bones have a rigid matrix containing calcium and phosphorus compounds. Cartilage has a soft, jelly-like matrix and provides flexibility and cushioning. Tendons connect muscles to bones, while ligaments connect bones to bones and prevent excessive movement.

The red colour of blood is due to haemoglobin, an iron-rich protein in the Red Blood Cells. RBCs live for about 4 months and are replaced regularly. Platelets help in blood clotting at the site of an injury. White Blood Cells collect at infected areas, causing pus formation and inflammation.

13Three kinds of muscleSkeletal, smooth and cardiac35 XP

Clench and open your fist. You chose to do that. Your heart has been beating the whole time and you chose nothing.

Movements under our conscious control, such as running, writing or lifting objects, are called voluntary movements and are carried out by skeletal muscles attached to the skeleton. They are made of bundles of long, cylindrical cells called muscle fibres, which are unbranched, multinucleate and striated, showing light and dark bands.

Movements that occur automatically, such as the movement of food in the intestine, are involuntary movements. Smooth muscles, found in organs like the stomach and intestines, are spindle-shaped, have a single nucleus and lack striations. Cardiac muscles are found only in the heart; their fibres are cylindrical and branched with a single nucleus and faint striations, and they work tirelessly and rhythmically without fatigue.

These actions are controlled by nervous tissue, which forms the body's control and coordination network. The brain acts as the control centre, coordinating activities, memory and responses across the body. Muscles, both voluntary and involuntary, cannot function independently — they receive instructions from the nervous tissue.

The cells of nervous tissue are called neurons, specialised to receive, process and transmit messages. Each neuron has three main parts: the cell body, which contains the nucleus and controls cell activities; dendrites, which receive signals from other neurons; and an axon, a long fibre that carries messages from the cell and ends at axon terminals, which transmit the messages to other cells.

Part 4 · Bones, joints and movement
15Which joint is that?Ball and socket, hinge, pivot, fixed35 XP

Circle your arm at the shoulder. Now try the same at the elbow. Shake your head 'no'. Now try to move a bone in your skull.

Choose the joint each movement needs.

A joint is a junction between two or more bones. In the ball and socket joint the rounded top of the upper arm bone fits into a shallow hollow of the shoulder bone, allowing forward, backward, sideways and circular movements. In a hinge joint, like the elbow and knee, the bone bends and straightens in one direction only, like a door hinge.

A pivot joint connects the skull to the backbone and allows the head to move side to side like a doorknob turning in its socket. Fixed joints connect the flat bones of the skull, which means those bones cannot move — keeping the brain safe even when the body moves.

The musculoskeletal system is made up of bones, muscles, joints, cartilage, tendons and ligaments, and functions under the control of the nervous system. Muscles pull on bones to produce movement. Joints allow movement but cannot move the bones on their own.

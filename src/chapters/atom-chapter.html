Part 1 · Where the idea came from
01Cut it in half, and againSection 8.1 · parmanu, atomos, and Dalton30 XP

Take something ordinary and halve it. Halve the half. Keep going. Acharya Kanada asked where this ends, and so did Leucippus and Democritus, two thousand years ago and half a world apart. Try it.

Start cutting. Each cut halves what is left. How many do you think it takes before there is nothing left to cut?

Kanada called the particle you finally reach a parmanu, too small for any sense to perceive, combining in twos and threes to build everything. Democritus called it atomos — Greek for indivisible. Both were reasoning, not experimenting. It was John Dalton, in 1808, who turned the idea into a scientific theory built on measurements.

Put the first stretch of the story in order, oldest first.
An idea before it was evidence
For most of its life the atom was philosophy, not physics. That matters: it is why the models in this chapter keep changing. Each one is the best picture that fitted the evidence available at the time, and each was replaced the moment better evidence arrived.
02Thomson finds the electronSection 8.2.1 · the cathode ray tube35 XP

In 1897 J. J. Thomson passed electricity through a gas at very low pressure and saw rays travel from the cathode to the anode. To find out what they were made of, he pushed them about with fields. Do the same.

The beam
travels straight
So the rays are
—
With no field the rays cross the tube in a straight line. Switch a field on and watch which way they bend.

The rays bend towards the positive plate, so they carry negative charge. Change the metal of the cathode, change the gas in the tube — the rays are identical every time. Whatever they are, every element contains them. Thomson had found the electron: a particle far lighter than any atom, and a piece of every atom there is.

Thomson now had a problem. Atoms are electrically neutral, but he had just shown they contain negative particles. What did he propose?

Thomson's model, often called the plum pudding model: a ball of positive charge with the electrons dotted through it, the positive exactly cancelling the negative. It was the first serious attempt to explain how an atom holds both charges and still reads neutral from the outside.
Part 2 · The experiment that broke the model

Keep firing. A bounce-back is rare — you may need a few hundred shots before you see one, which is exactly the problem Geiger and Marsden faced.

Almost everything goes straight through. A few particles turn sharply. Very occasionally one comes almost straight back. Rutherford said it was as astonishing as firing a shell at tissue paper and having it rebound at you — and the only way it can happen is if the atom's positive charge and nearly all its mass are packed into a nucleus far too small to hit often, with empty space everywhere else.

Why the rare event is the whole result
The particles that pass straight through tell you the atom is mostly empty. Only the ones that come back tell you what is at the centre. A result that happens once in a few thousand tries carried more information than all the others put together — which is why it had to be counted, one scintillation at a time, in a darkened room.

The nucleus is around one hundred thousand times smaller than the atom. Draw the atom the size of a cricket ground, about 100 m across, and the nucleus is a grain of black pepper on the pitch. Everything else — all the volume you think of as solid matter — is empty space with a few electrons in it.

A million atoms thick
A page of this textbook is about 0.1 mm thick. Divide that by the diameter of one atom and you get roughly a million. The paper you can barely feel between finger and thumb is a stack of a million atoms.
05Why this atom should collapseSection 8.2.2B · the limit of Rutherford's model35 XP

Rutherford's electrons circle the nucleus like planets round the Sun. But an electron moving in a circle is constantly changing direction, which means it is accelerating — and an accelerating charge radiates away its energy. Watch what that costs.

The electron loses energy as it goes, so its orbit shrinks. Nothing in Rutherford's model stops this.

If this really happened, every atom in the universe would have collapsed almost as soon as it formed — and matter would not exist. Atoms plainly do exist and are stable. So Rutherford's model, right about the nucleus, was missing something big.

Part 3 · Bohr's answer
06Fixed shellsSection 8.2.3 · stationary states and energy levels40 XP

In 1913 Niels Bohr fixed the problem by adding a rule: electrons may only occupy certain orbits, and while an electron stays in one of them it does not radiate. Move the electron between shells and watch what that costs or releases.

Now in
K · n=1
Energy
lowest
The jump
—
K is the shell closest to the nucleus and the lowest in energy. Send the electron outward and it must absorb energy; bring it inward and it releases the same amount.

The shells are labelled K, L, M, N… or numbered n = 1, 2, 3, 4. Energy rises as you move outward, and an electron may only ever hold one of these values — never anything in between. That single rule is what makes the atom stable, and it is why Bohr's model survived where Rutherford's could not.

Why K, and not A?
The names come from early X-ray work. Charles Barkla labelled the first series of lines he saw K rather than A, deliberately leaving room in case a series was later found beneath it. None ever was — but the gap in the alphabet stayed, and Bohr took the same letters for the shells.
07Three particles, three jobsTable 8.1 · and Chadwick's neutron, 193235 XP

A helium atom has two protons but weighs about four times a hydrogen atom, not twice. Something in the nucleus was adding mass without adding charge. In 1932 James Chadwick found it: the neutron, as heavy as a proton and electrically neutral.

Tick the box where the row and the column agree. Tap once for a tick, twice for a cross.

Protons and neutrons together are called nucleons, and they carry essentially all of an atom's mass. The electron is so light — about one two-thousandth of a proton — that it is simply ignored when mass is being counted.

What stops the nucleus flying apart?
Every proton in a nucleus repels every other proton, because they all carry positive charge. Neutrons help by getting in between, spacing the protons out and adding to the strong nuclear force that binds the whole thing. That is why heavy atoms need proportionally more neutrons: iron has 26 protons to 30 neutrons, while uranium needs 146 neutrons to hold 92 protons together.
Part 4 · Naming and counting
08Symbols of the elementsSection 8.4 · the ones that come from Latin30 XP

Dalton drew pictures for the elements. Berzelius replaced them in 1813 with letters taken from the element's Latin name, which is why a handful of symbols look nothing like the English word. These are the ones that catch people out.

Tap an element, then tap its symbol.

The rules are worth knowing: the first letter is always a capital and the second, if there is one, is always lower case — aluminium is Al, never AL. Some symbols take a letter from further into the name, like chlorine, Cl, and zinc, Zn. Today the names and symbols are approved internationally by IUPAC, so a chemist anywhere reads them the same way.

Atomic number Z is the number of protons, and it identifies the element — nothing else does. Mass number A is the number of protons plus neutrons, the total count of nucleons. Written together, the mass number goes above and the atomic number below, as in carbon-12.

Where the electrons went
A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons, so the electron count is Z as well. They are left out of the mass number because they contribute almost nothing to the mass — a proton is roughly 1836 times heavier than an electron.

Part 5 · Electrons in shells
11Build up an atomSection 8.7 · the 2n² rule, element by element45 XP

Add one electron at a time and watch where it has to go. A shell numbered n holds at most 2n² electrons — 2 in K, 8 in L, 18 in M — but no outermost shell ever holds more than 8, and a shell only starts filling once the one inside it is full.

Hydrogen's single electron goes into K, the shell nearest the nucleus and the lowest in energy.

The distribution of electrons across the shells is the atom's electronic configuration, and it is written innermost first: sodium is 2, 8, 1. Watch what happens at neon and at argon — the outermost shell reaches eight and the next electron has to start a whole new shell.

12ValencySection 8.8 · what an atom will do to reach an octet40 XP

An outermost shell holding eight electrons is an octet, and an atom with one is stable and largely unreactive. Everything else is trying to get there — by losing electrons, gaining them, or sharing. The number involved is the atom's valency.

Fewer than four electrons in the valence shell and it is cheaper to lose them: sodium is 2, 8, 1, so it loses one and has a valency of 1. More than four and it is cheaper to gain: oxygen is 2, 6, so it gains two and its valency is 2. Exactly four, like carbon, and it shares — valency 4.

The elements that do nothing
Helium (2), neon (2, 8) and argon (2, 8, 8) already have full outermost shells, so they have nothing to gain, lose or share. Their valency is 0, and they were the last elements to be discovered precisely because they refuse to react with anything.
Part 6 · Isotopes and isobars
13Twin atomsSection 8.9.1 · isotopes35 XP

Dalton thought every atom of an element was identical. He was almost right. Atoms of one element always have the same number of protons — but they can differ in neutrons, and so in mass. These are isotopes.

Choose an element to see its isotopes side by side. Count the protons in each, then count the neutrons.

All three hydrogen isotopes have one proton and one electron. Because chemistry is governed almost entirely by the electrons, isotopes of an element behave the same chemically — they differ in physical properties like melting and boiling point, and in whether they are radioactive.

14Average atomic massSection 8.9.1A · why chlorine is 35.540 XP

Chlorine comes in two isotopes, of mass 35 u and 37 u. So what is the mass of "a chlorine atom"? Take the plain average of 35 and 37 and you get 36 — but nature does not supply them in equal numbers. Slide the abundance and watch the two answers separate.

Weighted mean
35.5 u
Working
—
The simple mean never moves, because it ignores how common each isotope actually is. The weighted mean follows the abundance.

Chlorine-35 makes up about 75% of natural chlorine and chlorine-37 about 25%, so the weighted average atomic mass is (35 × 75/100) + (37 × 25/100) = 35.5 u. No single chlorine atom weighs 35.5 u — take a million of them and you get roughly 750 000 of one and 250 000 of the other.

15Isotopes, isobars or neither?Section 8.9.2 · telling the pairs apart35 XP

Two atoms with the same atomic number and different mass numbers are isotopes — same element, different neutron count. Two atoms with the same mass number and different atomic numbers are isobars — different elements that happen to have the same total of nucleons.

The story does not end here
Bohr's neat orbits turned out not to be right either. Electrons do not follow fixed paths; the modern model describes clouds of probability, regions where an electron is likely to be rather than places it definitely is.

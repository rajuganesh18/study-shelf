Part 1 · Seeing an identity
01The answer is always 2Section 4.1 · Example 140 XP

Take any three consecutive square numbers. Add the smallest and the largest, then subtract twice the middle one. Try it with several starting points before reading on.

The three squares
—
Smallest + largest
—
Minus 2 × middle
—
1, 4, 9 gives 1 + 9 − 8 = 2. Try 9, 16, 25. Try 25, 36, 49. Then ask why it never changes.

Write the three numbers as n − 1, n and n + 1. Their squares add as (n − 1)² + (n + 1)² = n² − 2n + 1 + n² + 2n + 1 = 2n² + 2, and subtracting 2n² leaves 2 — with no n in it at all. That is why the starting point never matters, and it is the first thing in this chapter that an identity explains rather than merely describes.

02The square of side a + bSection 4.2 · Fig. 4.255 XP

Take a line of length a and add a length b to it. Build the square on that line, and cut it where the two lengths meet. Four pieces fall out, and their areas are the identity.

(a + b)²
—
a² + 2ab + b²
—
Equal?
—
The outer square and the four pieces are the same area, whatever a and b you choose.

The outer square has area (a + b)². Inside it sit a square of area a², a square of area b², and two rectangles of area ab each. Nothing is left over and nothing overlaps, so (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² — proved by a picture, for every pair of lengths.

03But lengths are only part of the storySection 4.2 · Example 235 XP

The picture proves the identity for lengths, and a length cannot be negative. So does the rule survive when a and b are negative, or fractions? Work both sides out and compare.

It survives every time, and the reason is not the picture — it is the distributive property: (a + b)² = (a + b)(a + b) = a(a + b) + b(a + b) = a² + ab + ba + b² = a² + 2ab + b². That argument never mentions length, so it holds for every number there is.

04Identity, or just an equation?Section 4.2 · the definition35 XP

An identity is true for every value of its variables. An equation may only be true for some. Sort these.

x² − 1 = 24 is true only when x = 5 or x = −5, so it is an equation you can solve. (x + y)² = x² + 2xy + y² is true no matter what you put in, so there is nothing to solve — it is a rule you can use. Every identity is an equation; almost no equation is an identity.

Part 2 · Using the two square identities
05Which is bigger?Section 4.2 · Think and Reflect40 XP

(a + b)² and a² + b² are not the same thing. Which is larger depends on one term only — find it.

(a + b)²
—
a² + b²
—
The difference
—
Find a case where the left is bigger, one where the right is, and one where they are equal.

The gap between them is exactly 2ab, so the sign of ab decides everything. If a and b have the same sign then 2ab is positive and (a + b)² is the larger; if their signs differ, 2ab is negative and a² + b² wins; and they are equal precisely when ab = 0, which means at least one of them is zero.

06Expand with the identityExercise Set 4.1 · Example 340 XP

Every one of these is (a + b)² in disguise. Name a and b, then write the three terms.

The book's own case is (5x + 2y)², where a = 5x and b = 2y, giving (5x)² + 2(5x)(2y) + (2y)² = 25x² + 20xy + 4y². The identity does not care that a and b are themselves expressions — that is exactly what makes it worth having.

07The square with a bite taken outSection 4.3 · Fig. 4.350 XP

Replace b by −b in the first identity and you get (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b². Here is the same result as a picture: start from the big square of side a and take pieces away.

(a − b)²
—
a² − 2ab + b²
—
What was removed
—
Take the rectangle ab off the side and b(a − b) off the bottom. What is left is the little square.

The big square is a². Remove a rectangle of area ab and another of area b(a − b), and the pale square of side (a − b) is what remains: a² − ab − b(a − b) = a² − ab − ba + b² = a² − 2ab + b². The two removed pieces overlap in nothing, which is why nothing has to be added back.

08Squaring numbers in your headExamples 4, 8 and 945 XP

Work through one that adds, one that subtracts, and the three-part one.

43² is (40 + 3)² = 1600 + 240 + 9 = 1849. 29² is easier as (30 − 1)² = 900 − 60 + 1 = 841. And 119² needs the three-term identity: (100 + 10 + 9)² = 10000 + 100 + 81 + 2000 + 1800 + 180 = 14161. Choosing the right split is the whole skill.

09Run the identity backwardsExamples 5 to 7 · Exercise Set 4.240 XP

Read a² + 2ab + b² from right to left and it becomes a way of factoring. Spot the two squares, check the middle term is twice their roots multiplied, and the factors fall out.

The check that matters is the middle term. In 36x² + 12x + 1 the outer terms are (6x)² and 1², and 2 × 6x × 1 = 12x — which matches, so it really is (6x + 1)². If the middle term had been anything else, no amount of wanting would make it a perfect square.

10Three terms, nine piecesSection 4.4 · Fig. 4.450 XP

What if the side is a + b + c? Cut the square along both directions and count what falls out.

(a + b + c)²
—
Three squares
—
Six rectangles
—
Nine pieces: three squares on the diagonal, and six rectangles in matching pairs.

Along the diagonal sit the squares a², b² and c². Off it sit six rectangles in three matching pairs — two of area ab, two of bc, two of ca. So (a + b + c)² = a² + b² + c² + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca. The book gets there by algebra, putting d = b + c in the two-term identity; the picture says the same thing at a glance.

Part 3 · Algebra tiles, and splitting the middle term
11Laying out algebra tilesSection 4.5 · Fig. 4.750 XP

One big tile of area x², some thin tiles of area x, and some unit squares. Arrange them into a rectangle and its two side lengths are the factors.

The rectangle
—
Tiles used
—
Its area
—
Build the book's rectangle, x + 3 by x + 4, then try others.

The x-tiles split into two groups: 3 stacked beside the x² tile and 4 laid beneath it, which is why the 7x in x² + 7x + 12 appears as 3x + 4x. The 12 unit tiles fill the corner in a 3 by 4 array — and 3 × 4 = 12 is not a coincidence, it is the identity (x + a)(x + b) = x² + (a + b)x + ab made out of cardboard.

12Splitting the middle termSection 4.6 · Examples 10 to 1245 XP

Without tiles, factoring x² + bx + c means finding two numbers that add to b and multiply to c. Both conditions at once, or neither counts.

Their sum
—
Their product

Verdict
—
Solve three of these. Both conditions have to hold together.

For x² + 11x + 30 the book tries a = 2, b = 15 and a = 3, b = 10: both multiply to 30, and neither adds to 11. Only 5 and 6 satisfy both, so 11x splits as 5x + 6x and the factors are (x + 5)(x + 6). When the middle term is negative but the constant positive — x² − 5x + 6 — both numbers must be negative: −2 and −3.

13Fill in the factorsExercise Set 4.4 · Q135 XP

Complete each factorisation.

The last two have a coefficient in front of x², so the split has to account for it: in 6x² + 7x + 2 you need two numbers multiplying to 6 × 2 = 12 and adding to 7 — that is 3 and 4 — giving 6x² + 3x + 4x + 2 and then (2x + 1)(3x + 2). Always multiply out to check; it takes seconds and catches every sign slip.

Part 4 · Into the third dimension
14The cube of a + bSection 4.7 · Figs. 4.9 and 4.1055 XP

A square of side a + b split into four pieces. What does a cube of edge a + b split into? Cut it in all three directions and count.

(a + b)³
—
The eight pieces
—
Equal?
—
Eight boxes: two cubes and six cuboids, which group into four terms.

Two of the eight are cubes, of volume a³ and b³. Of the remaining six, three measure a × a × b and three measure a × b × b, so they total 3a²b and 3ab². Hence (a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³. Replacing b by −b flips the sign of every term with an odd number of b's, giving (a − b)³ = a³ − 3a²b + 3ab² − b³, with the signs alternating.

15Cancelling a common factorSection 4.8 · Example 1645 XP

A rational expression simplifies only when the top and the bottom share a factor — and you cannot see a shared factor until both are factored. Work through it step by step.

The book's case is (x² − 7x + 12) over (5x² + 5x − 100). The top factors as (x − 3)(x − 4). The bottom has a common factor of 5, leaving 5(x² + x − 20) = 5(x − 4)(x + 5). The shared (x − 4) cancels — and it is allowed to, precisely because we were told the denominator is not zero — leaving (x − 3) / 5(x + 5).

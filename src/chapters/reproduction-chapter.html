Part 1 · One parent is enough

Every one of these is a vegetative part — a stem, a root or a leaf. None of them is a seed.

New plants arising from the vegetative parts of a parent — its growing stems, roots and leaves — is vegetative propagation. Fleshy underground stems like potato and ginger sprout new plants without producing seeds; money plant and sugarcane stem cuttings grow into new plants; Bryophyllum leaves sprout tiny plantlets along their notched margins.

The key point is that only one parent is involved, so the offspring are genetically identical to it. They are clones. That is the defining feature of asexual reproduction, and everything that follows in this part is a variation on it.

Cutting takes a length of shoot, strips the lower leaves, and plants it at about 45–60° in soil mixed with compost. Grafting joins a stem piece of one plant (the scion) into a slit cut in a healthy rooted plant of another variety (the stock), then binds the wound until it heals — one root system, another variety's shoot. Layering buries the middle of a flexible twig while it is still attached to the parent; after 10–15 days roots develop, and only then is the twig cut free.

Why a banana farm is grown in a jar
Tissue culture takes cells from the shoot tip of a plant and grows thousands of identical plantlets on a nutrient medium. Farmers get mass-produced healthy plantlets, which helps eliminate virus-infected stock and raises yields. It is the same asexual principle, done at industrial scale.

In yeast, repeated cell division at one site produces a bud that enlarges and separates to live independently — this is budding. Hydra, a multicellular animal, does exactly the same, often with several buds on one parent at once. Moulds instead form spores inside a sac at the tip of a hypha (Rhizopus) or on a swollen vesicle (Aspergillus). Spores are light, usually single-celled, and drift on air currents until they find moisture and nutrients.

Why the fridge works
Spores need warmth (25–35 °C) and moisture to grow. Lower temperatures slow or stop their reproduction, which is why we refrigerate perishable food. Before refrigerators became common about a hundred years ago, fresh food lasted one or two days.

For a long time people thought living things arose spontaneously from non-living matter. Louis Pasteur showed by experiment that new life always comes from pre-existing life — which gave us the germ theory of disease, strengthened the cell theory, and led to sterilising instruments.

04Which organism, which method?Section 11.1 · sorting the asexual routes35 XP

All of these are asexual — one parent, identical offspring — but they get there by different routes.

Whatever the route, the cell division behind all of it is mitosis: one cell becomes two daughter cells, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent. That is why the offspring are clones. It is fast, and it lets a population grow quickly when conditions are favourable.

Part 2 · Two parents, and the reason for them

Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes — 46 in total, one of each pair from each parent.

Meiosis is a special type of cell division that forms gametes. It halves the chromosome number: a diploid parent cell (46 in humans) gives haploid daughter cells with 23. In animals the male gametes are sperm and the female gametes are eggs; in plants the pollen grain carries the male gametes to an ovule, which holds the female gamete.

During meiosis the two chromosomes of each pair separate, so every gamete receives exactly one chromosome from each pair. Two gametes fuse and the full count is restored. The number stays fixed generation after generation.

A human zygote has just formed. How many chromosomes does it have?
The zygote is the fusion of two gametes carrying 23 each, so it has 46 — the same as every other body cell. It is the gametes, not the zygote, that carry the halved number.

With three pairs of characters, eight combinations are possible. Humans have 23 pairs, each carrying information for many characters — so the number of possible gametes runs into millions before you even consider that two such gametes then combine at random.

This random mixing is what makes children genetically different from their parents and from their siblings. Each of us receives a unique combination of chromosomes. That variation is the whole point: it helps some individuals cope better with changing environments, and over time it is what allows populations to adapt and evolve. Some people tolerate low oxygen at high altitudes; some digest milk in adulthood.

Asexual reproduction is fast, needs no partner, and reliably reproduces a plant whose qualities you already like — which is exactly why a farmer propagating a high-yield mango uses grafting rather than seeds. The cost is that a whole field of clones shares a single set of weaknesses; one disease that defeats one plant defeats all of them.

Part 3 · The flower

A complete flower has four parts. The sepals are the green covering of the outermost whorl, which protects the flower in the bud stage. The petals are the coloured projections. The stamen is the male part: a filament carrying an anther, which produces pollen grains containing the male gametes. The pistil is the female part, with three subparts — the stigma at the tip, often flat or sticky; the style, a thin tube; and the ovary at the base, containing ovules, each with an egg cell.

Not every flower has all four. Papaya bears male and female flowers on different trees; in some flowers the sepals and petals are fused. A flower missing its stamens cannot make pollen, and one missing its pistil cannot make seeds.

Work through all five. One of them fails, and the reason it fails is the whole point of the experiment.

Fruits form in every treatment except the one where the stamens were removed from the flower bud — before the flower ever opened, and so before any of its own pollen could be shed onto its own stigma. From that single failure we can infer that the transfer of pollen from anther to stigma is necessary for fruit formation. That transfer is pollination.

Pea flowers pollinate themselves inside the closed bud, which is why treatment 3 still sets fruit even though its stamens were removed and it was bagged: by the time the flower had opened, the job was already done.

10Pollen to zygote, in orderSection 11.2.5 · Fig. 11.1430 XP

Once pollen reaches a compatible stigma, a chain of events follows. Put them in order.

The pollen grain germinates on the stigma and grows a pollen tube down through the style into the ovary. The male gamete travels through this tube and reaches the ovule, where it fuses with the egg cell. That fusion is fertilisation, and it marks the beginning of a new life. The fertilised egg is a zygote, which develops into an embryo.

Meanwhile the ovary enlarges into a fruit and the ovules inside it become seeds. Seeds are dispersed by wind, water or animals — the plumed seeds of madar and dandelion are shaped for wind. When water, air and temperature are favourable, the seed germinates into a new plant.

Pollination is self-pollination when pollen reaches the stigma of the same flower or another flower of the same plant, and cross-pollination when it reaches a flower of a different plant of the same kind. Cross-pollination mixes genetic material from two individuals; self-pollination does not, so a plant that only ever self-pollinates loses genetic diversity generation by generation.

Wind pollination is enormously wasteful per grain — thousands of grains for every seed — because the wind has no idea where it is going. Insect pollination is far more efficient per grain because the insect delivers the pollen to another flower of the same kind on purpose, but the plant pays for that service in nectar, scent and colour.

In many aquatic animals such as frogs and most fish, the female releases eggs into the water and the male releases sperm over them — external fertilisation. Enormous numbers are laid, and most are swept away or eaten. In reptiles, birds and mammals fertilisation happens inside the female's body — internal fertilisation — and the fertilised egg or embryo is far better protected, so far fewer are needed.

The yolk in an egg tells the same story. Fish, amphibians and insects put just enough yolk in each egg to produce a larva, which hatches and then feeds for itself — the caterpillar stage of a butterfly is exactly this, a feeding stage that accumulates nutrition before the transformation into an adult. Reptiles and birds pack enough yolk to nourish the embryo all the way to hatching. In mammals the zygote develops inside the female body, and the young are fed on milk after birth.

Part 6 · Reproduction in human beings

Sperm are produced in two oval organs called testes, held in a pouch of skin, the scrotum, which keeps them slightly cooler than the rest of the body — a requirement for sperm formation. Sperm travel through the vas deferens, which opens into the urethra. The seminal vesicles and prostate add fluids that nourish the sperm and keep them motile. The testes also produce a hormone that controls sperm production and the physical changes of puberty.

The female system has a pair of ovaries, which produce eggs and release hormones; a pair of oviducts or fallopian tubes connecting each ovary to the uterus; the uterus, a bag-like structure where a foetus develops; and the cervix, a narrow passage through which the uterus opens into the vagina.

The formation of gametes is called gametogenesis, and it happens in the testes and ovaries. Body cells have 46 chromosomes but gametes have only 23, so that a zygote formed by two of them has the same 46 as its parents. In males gametogenesis produces very large numbers of tiny motile sperm; in females it produces a single large egg with stored nutrients and no motility of its own. This asymmetry between male and female gametes holds across most of the animal kingdom.

Who determines the sex of a baby
Every person has two sex chromosomes: females XX, males XY. The mother can only ever contribute an X. The father contributes either an X, giving XX, or a Y, giving XY. So it is the father's gamete that settles it — which is worth knowing, because the opposite belief has done a great deal of harm.

Days 1–5 the lining sheds; days 6–14 it rebuilds while an egg matures; around day 14 the egg is released; days 15–28 the lining thickens and waits.

Ovulation is the release of a mature egg from an ovary, around day 14 of a typical cycle. The egg travels into the oviduct. If sperm are present, one may fuse with it there, forming a zygote. The zygote divides as it travels to the uterus and implants into the thickened lining, which is the beginning of pregnancy — and the reason the cycle stops during pregnancy, because the lining is now needed and is no longer shed.

If the egg is not fertilised it remains viable for about a day and then degenerates. The thickened lining is no longer needed, so it sheds, and this — along with some blood — leaves the body through the vagina. That is menstruation, usually lasting three to seven days. The whole cycle typically repeats every 21–35 days, often around 28, and begins at puberty between about 10 and 14 years, continuing until menopause at around 50.

A student claims: “In humans, ovulation always happens on day 14 of the cycle.” Is the claim sound?
Day 14 is the midpoint of a typical 28-day cycle, not a rule. Cycle length varies between people and between months for the same person, and cycles can be irregular — especially in the years just after they begin. The textbook's own figure says "typically every 21–35 days (often around 28)".

Pregnancy in humans lasts about nine months, divided into three trimesters. In the first, the fertilised egg develops into an embryo and the major organs begin forming; from about the ninth week it is called a foetus. In the second, the foetus grows stronger and its movements can usually be felt. In the third, it grows rapidly and gets ready for life outside. During childbirth, strong contractions of the uterus push the baby out through the birth canal.

Menstruation is a sign of health
It is a sign of a healthy reproductive system, not something to be ashamed of. Use clean menstrual products and change them every four to six hours, or more often if the flow is heavy. Wash the genital area with water — soap can disrupt the natural bacterial balance — and wash hands with soap before and after changing a pad. Wrap used products in paper and bin them; never flush them.

Sexual maturity — sperm production in boys, menstrual cycles in girls — arrives gradually during adolescence, but emotional maturity takes longer. Being ready for adult decisions means being able to handle feelings, communicate clearly and think things through. Responsible choices at this stage help prevent unplanned pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) such as gonorrhoea, herpes, syphilis, genital warts and HIV, some of which are not yet curable. Condoms reduce STI transmission and also help prevent pregnancy.

Contraceptive methods include barriers such as condoms; oral pills that alter hormones to change the release of eggs, though these can have side effects; and intra-uterine devices such as the copper-T, which may sometimes irritate the uterus. Surgical methods block the vas deferens or the fallopian tubes so that sperm and egg cannot meet. In India, prenatal sex determination is prohibited by law, because sex-selective abortion driven by a preference for a particular gender distorts the sex ratio of the whole society.

Part 1 · How likely is it?
01The scale from 0 to 1Section 7.1.2 · Fig. 7.140 XP

Probability is a measurement, like length or area. What it measures is how likely something is, and the whole of it fits between 0 and 1. Here is the book's own deck of six cards, with the number of purple ones up to you.

Take the purple cards from none to all six, and watch the marker travel the whole scale.

At 0 the event is impossible — there is no purple card to draw. At 1 it is certain. Halfway, at 0.5, purple and green are equally likely, which is what a fair coin gives you. Everything else is a matter of degree, and that is the point of using a number rather than a word: 0.75 and 0.8 are both “more likely”, but one of them is more likely than the other.

02Put each event on the scaleSection 7.1.2 · Exercise Set 7.145 XP

Six events, five bands. Decide where each one belongs.

Two of these deserve a second look. A card numbered 2 to 10 from a deck of 52 is more likely rather than certain: there are 36 such cards, so it is 36⁄52, a little over two-thirds — high, but far from a promise. And rolling a 3 is less likely rather than impossible: one face in six is unlikely, but it happens all the time. Impossible means it cannot happen at all, and there is a real difference.

Before you can measure a chance you have to know what the possibilities are. The full list is the sample space, written S, and how many things are in it is the sample size, n(S).

Two rules make a list a sample space: it must contain every possible outcome, and no outcome twice. That multiplication is doing a lot of work later in this chapter.

Part 2 · Counting the ways
04Favourable over possibleSection 7.2.2 · Examples 3 and 450 XP

Pick an experiment, then an event. The highlighted tiles are the favourable ones — the fraction is just counting them.

P = favourable ÷ possible. Rolling a 4 on a die is 1⁄6 ≈ 0.167; the letter B in PROBABILITY is 2⁄11 ≈ 0.182, because that word has eleven letters and two of them are B. Notice the two ends: an event with no favourable outcomes has probability 0, and one where every outcome is favourable has probability 1. The whole scale from mission 1 falls out of this one fraction.

05Roll it until the answer appearsSections 7.2.1 and 7.2.3 · the Law of Large Numbers55 XP

The other way to measure a probability is to go and find out. Roll the die, keep score, and divide: that is experimental probability, also called the relative frequency.

Roll a handful and the answer is all over the place. Roll a few hundred and it walks in towards 0.1667.

Roll ten times and getting three 4s is nothing unusual — that is an experimental probability of 0.3, nearly double the truth. Roll a thousand times and a result that far out would be astonishing. This is the Law of Large Numbers: the experimental probability does not have to match the theoretical one, but the more trials you run the harder it becomes for them to stay apart. It is a statement about the long run, and about nothing else.

The rate lands on 1⁄6 whatever run you searched for, because the die is not keeping score. Believing otherwise is the Gambler's Fallacy, and it is worth being precise about what is and is not true. A run of four sixes is rare — before you start rolling, its chance is 1⁄1296. But once the first three have happened they are facts, not chances, and the only thing still uncertain is one ordinary roll.

07Is every outcome equally likely?End-of-chapter Q3 · fair and unbiased40 XP

The favourable-over-possible rule has a condition attached, and it is easy to forget: every outcome has to be equally likely. Which of these qualify?

Two outcomes is not the same as two equal outcomes. A car either starts or it does not, but a car in good repair starts almost every time — the two outcomes are not equally likely, so you cannot say the chance is a half. The same trap catches the bag of 3 red and 7 blue marbles: two colours, but seven chances against three. When the book calls a coin fair and a die unbiased, that assumption is what is being made, and it is exactly what lets you count instead of experiment.

Part 3 · Working the numbers
08From a sample to a whole schoolSection 7.2.3 · Example 5 and Exercise Set 7.255 XP

You cannot ask 1500 students what fruit they like. You ask 50, work out the fractions, and scale up. That is how probability earns its keep outside a classroom.

Estimate for all

Pick a survey, then a category. The estimate is the sample fraction applied to the whole population.

Twenty of fifty students said mango, so P(mango) = 0.4, and 40% of 1500 is 600 mangoes to buy. Everything here rests on the sample being representative — fifty students from one class may not speak for the school, and a bigger, better-spread sample is what makes the estimate worth trusting. That is the job of sampling, and mission 15 is about how much it matters.

09Work the probabilitiesEnd-of-chapter Q4, Q8, Q950 XP

Six events. Write down the sample space in your head, count the favourable outcomes, and divide.

The three-coin rows are the ones that catch people out, because it is tempting to think the sample space is {0 heads, 1 head, 2 heads, 3 heads} — four outcomes, so a quarter each. It is not: the real sample space is the eight sequences HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT, and those are the equally likely things. Exactly two heads happens in three of them, so 3⁄8 and not 1⁄4.

Part 4 · Two steps, and the tree that shows them
11Every path on a treeSection 7.4 · Example 7 and Fig. 7.645 XP

Toss a coin twice and there are four ways it can go. Toss it four times and there are sixteen. A tree diagram lays every one of them out so that none is missed and none is counted twice.

n(S)
—
Each single path
—
P(that many heads)
—
Every path from the root to a leaf is one complete outcome, and every path is equally likely.

Each toss doubles the number of leaves, so n(S) = 2n and every single path has probability 1⁄2n. Getting HH from two tosses is therefore 1⁄4 — and getting one head and one tail is 2⁄4 = 1⁄2, because two different paths, HT and TH, both count. That is the answer to the book's Think and Reflect, and it is the first sign that counting paths is not the same as counting results.

With 4 red and 5 blue and no replacement, P(red then blue) = 4⁄9 × 5⁄8 = 20⁄72 = 5⁄18. Put the ball back and the second denominator stays at 9, giving 4⁄9 × 5⁄9 = 20⁄81 instead. The first draw changed what the bag contained, so it changed the second probability — the two draws are no longer independent, which is precisely the difference between this mission and the coin tosses in mission 11.

Part 5 · Probability out in the world
14Drop a dye on the boardEnd-of-chapter Q16 · Fig. 7.855 XP

Not every sample space is a list you can write out. Drop a spot of dye at random on a 3 m by 2 m rectangle and there are infinitely many places it could land — so the counting is done with area instead, which is the last chapter's work put to a new use.

Landed inside

Drop a few thousand spots and the fraction inside the circle walks in towards the ratio of the areas.

P = area of the circle ÷ area of the rectangle. With a diameter of 1 m that is π × 0.5² ÷ 6 = π⁄24 ≈ 0.1309, which is the book's answer. Notice what the drops are doing: they are measuring π. Run enough of them and you could work out π to a couple of decimal places without ever drawing a polygon — which is a very different route to the same constant Archimedes squeezed by hand.

Typical error
—
Take samples of ten, then of five hundred. The dots do not move — they narrow.

Every one of these samples is honest and unbiased; none of them is doing anything wrong. They still disagree, and with only ten students they disagree wildly — 0.2 and 0.6 are both perfectly ordinary answers. Quadrupling the sample size halves the typical error, so getting twice as accurate costs four times the work. That is why a survey reports how many people it asked, and why “we asked ten people” is not a finding.

The sealed flask is the honest one. In the open flask the carbon dioxide simply leaves, and the balance reports the loss — but the gas still exists, and it still has mass. Trap it and the reading does not move by a single milligram.

Why the balloon has to stay on the pan
The book is insistent about leaving both the flask and the balloon on the balance throughout. Traces of baking soda cling to the inside of the balloon, and lifting it off to tip the powder in would take some of that mass away with it — producing a "loss" that has nothing to do with chemistry.
02Is the law broken?Section 9.1 · Lavoisier, 178935 XP

Matter can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. That is the Law of Conservation of Mass, and every apparent exception turns out to be something entering or leaving the container unnoticed. Decide each case.

Lavoisier's own words
"…in every operation an equal quantity of matter exists both before and after the operation." Antoine Lavoisier is called the father of modern chemistry largely because he insisted on weighing everything, including the gases — which is exactly the discipline the balloon enforces.

Part 2 · Always the same recipe
04Water is always one to eightSection 9.2 · the Law of Constant Proportions40 XP

Purify water from a river, a borewell or the ocean, decompose it, and you always get hydrogen and oxygen in a mass ratio of 1 : 8. Try to force a different ratio and see what happens to the leftovers.

One gram of hydrogen and eight of oxygen make nine grams of water with nothing to spare. Now try supplying too much of one of them.

Whichever element runs out first decides how much compound forms. The surplus of the other is simply left sitting there unreacted — it cannot force its way into the water, because the proportion is fixed.

Cinnabar, and a very old ratio
The red pigment known in India as hingula and in Latin as cinnabar yields mercury and sulfur at about 86.22% and 13.78% by mass — and grinding the two together in that same ratio produces cinnabar again. Several civilisations found this independently, long before anyone could say why.
05Working with fixed ratiosExample 9.3 and the Pause and Ponder questions35 XP

Once you know a compound's mass ratio, every other quantity follows from it. Six to work out.

Why it does not apply to mixtures
Air is roughly a fifth oxygen — but only roughly, and it varies with place and altitude. A mixture has no fixed recipe, which is precisely what separates it from a compound. Two students who make copper oxide in the ratios 4:1 and 8:2 have in fact made the same compound: those ratios are equal.
06Dalton's postulatesSection 9.3 · what he got right, and what he did not30 XP

In 1808 John Dalton explained both laws at once with a handful of assumptions about atoms. A postulate is a starting assumption, accepted without proof, from which everything else is reasoned.

Tick every statement that Dalton actually postulated.

The theory explains the two laws neatly. Mass is conserved because atoms are merely rearranged, never created or destroyed. Proportions are fixed because a given compound always contains the same kinds of atoms in the same whole-number ratio.

Two postulates that did not survive
Chapter 8 broke both of them. Atoms are not indivisible — they contain protons, neutrons and electrons. And atoms of one element are not all identical in mass, because isotopes exist. Dalton was wrong about the details and right about the consequences, which is a very common shape for a good scientific theory.

One shared pair is a single bond, drawn as one line: H—H, Cl—Cl. Two shared pairs make a double bond, O=O. Three make a triple bond, as in nitrogen — which is why nitrogen gas is so hard to persuade into reacting with anything.

Why neon does nothing
Neon already has eight electrons in its outermost shell. It has nothing to gain, nothing to give away and no reason to share — so it forms no bonds at all and drifts about as single atoms. Helium does the same with a full shell of two.
09Naming covalent compoundsSection 9.4.1C · mono, di, tri, tetra35 XP

Covalent names count the atoms. The first element keeps its name, the second ends in -ide, and prefixes say how many: mono- 1, di- 2, tri- 3, tetra- 4, penta- 5, hexa- 6. Mono- is dropped from the first element but kept on the second.

Tap a formula, then tap its name.

Two spelling rules catch people out. If the prefix ends in o or a and the element starts with a vowel, the last vowel is dropped — carbon monoxide, not monooxide; dinitrogen tetroxide, not tetraoxide. If it ends in i it stays, for pronunciation: dioxide, trioxide.

The ones nobody calls by their proper name
H₂O really ought to be dihydrogen monoxide, and NH₃ nitrogen trihydride. Everyone says water and ammonia instead. A few compounds were named long before the system existed and simply kept the names they had.

Both atoms start electrically neutral, with as many electrons as protons. Press transfer and count again.

Sodium keeps its 11 protons but is down to 10 electrons, so it now carries one unit of positive charge: the cation Na⁺. Chlorine has 17 protons and 18 electrons, so it carries one negative: the anion Cl⁻. Opposite charges attract, and that electrostatic pull is the ionic bond.

So NaCl is not a molecular formula at all — it is the simplest whole-number ratio of the ions in the lattice. That is why an ionic compound has a formula unit rather than a molecule, and why we speak of its formula unit mass rather than its molecular mass.

When the brackets are needed
Magnesium hydroxide is Mg(OH)₂, not MgOH₂ — the subscript has to apply to the whole hydroxide group, not just the hydrogen. But aluminium hydroxide with one nitrate needs no brackets at all: brackets appear only when a polyatomic ion is taken more than once.
14Telling them apartActivity 9.4 · ionic against covalent40 XP

Take camphor, common salt, copper sulfate, sugar and naphthalene. Try to dissolve each in water and in kerosene, then test whether it conducts. The results sort themselves into two clear groups.

Tick where the statement is true of that kind of compound. Tap once for a tick, twice for a cross.

Ionic solids do not conduct, because their ions are locked in the lattice and cannot move. Dissolve them and the ions come free, and the bulb lights. Covalent compounds mostly dissolve in kerosene and petrol rather than water, and give no ions at all — so no conduction either way.

What about melting them?
Melting an ionic solid frees the ions just as dissolving does, so molten sodium chloride conducts too. It takes a great deal of heat to get there: the inter-ionic attraction is strong, which is why ionic compounds have high melting points and covalent ones generally have low ones.

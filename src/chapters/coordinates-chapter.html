Part 1 · Why anyone needed coordinates
01Grid thinking is oldSection 1.1 · where the idea came from40 XP

The Cartesian plane carries one European name, but the thinking behind it was assembled over thousands of years and across several civilisations. Put these in the order they happened.

The first systematic use of grids happened on a massive urban scale in the Sindhu–Sarasvatī Civilisation, where city streets ran North–South and East–West at uniform distances of about 10 metres. That is a coordinate system in practice: a merchant could find a warehouse by counting North–South and East–West units from the city centre.

Baudhāyana used East–West and North–South lines for his geometric constructions, developing the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem — the very result this chapter uses to measure distance. Ujjayinī was the point marking the central longitude meridian from which all other locations were measured, later entering Arabic geography as ‘Arin’.

Why zero and the negatives mattered
Brahmagupta (c. 628 CE) formalised zero and negative numbers as algebraic entities. In a modern coordinate system the origin is zero and the negative axes represent values less than zero. Without that work the four-quadrant plane could not exist — you will take three of its quadrants away in Mission 4 and see what is left.

Part 3 · Distance between two points
08Distance along a lineSection 1.4 · segments parallel to an axis40 XP

Start with the easy case. When two points share the same y, the segment joining them is parallel to the x-axis, and the distance is just the difference of the x-coordinates — taken positively, whichever way round you subtract.

Make the difference negative, then positive, then zero. The distance never goes below zero.

The distance between (x₁, y) and (x₂, y) is the absolute value |x₂ − x₁|. The same argument along the other axis gives |y₂ − y₁| for (x, y₁) and (x, y₂). Direction is a matter of which one you subtract from which; distance is not.

09The triangle hiding under every segmentSection 1.4 · Fig. 1.7 · triangle ADM55 XP

Reproduce all three sides of the book's triangle ADM: AD, DM and MA.

In general, the distance between (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) is √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]. It makes no difference whether the two differences come out positive or negative — they are squared, and squaring destroys the sign. That is the same fact as the absolute value in the previous mission, wearing different clothes.

Why the legs are always parallel to the axes
The construction drops from A straight down and runs from there straight across to D. Those two directions are perpendicular by the definition of the axes, so the triangle is right-angled by construction — you never have to check it. This is why coordinates make geometry calculable: the right angle is built into the paper.

A circle is simply the set of points at one fixed distance from a centre — so the distance formula decides membership. Circle K is centred at the origin and passes through A (1, −8), B (−4, 7) and C (−7, −4). Move a test point and let the formula rule.

Find a point inside the circle, one exactly on it, and one outside.

Each of A, B and C is √65 from the origin: 1 + 64, 16 + 49 and 49 + 16 all come to 65. So the radius is √65, a little over 8. D (−5, 6) gives 25 + 36 = 61 < 65 and lies inside; E (0, 9) gives 81 > 65 and lies outside. You never have to take a square root to decide — comparing the squares is enough.

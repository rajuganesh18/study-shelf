From at least the second millennium BCE we begin to have literary sources — the Ṛigveda being the earliest — alongside the archaeology. That is why our picture of this period is so much richer than the one before it: texts tell us about ideas, institutions and practices that pots and post-holes cannot.

The archaeology still does a great deal of work. In the first millennium BCE the Ganga region shows increasing evidence of iron implements, a wider variety of grain, and the characteristic Painted Grey Ware. In the second half of that millennium, particularly in the middle Ganga from present-day Prayagraj to eastern Bihar, a refined 'deluxe ware' appears — Northern Black Polished Ware, smooth and glossy black. Further south, megalithic burials mark the same iron-using centuries.

The Vedas are the earliest known body of Indian literature — which matters all the more because the Harappan script has still not been deciphered. Vedic literature existed in oral form for several centuries before it was written down. The date of the Ṛigveda's composition is debated among historians, with estimates ranging from the fifth millennium BCE to the second.

Each Veda has four parts. The samhitā holds the hymns used to invoke a deity and offer oblations in the yajña. The brāhmaṇa, in prose, explains how the rituals are performed. The āraṇyaka — from araṇya, forest — expands on the philosophical speculations of sages living in the forest. The upaniṣhad goes into the Self (Ātman) and the Ultimate Universal Being (Brāhmaṇ).

Three words that look alike
Brāhmaṇ is a concept — the Ultimate Universal Being. Brāhmaṇas are texts — the prose explanations of ritual. And brāhmaṇas are also a varṇa category. Keep the three apart and a lot of confusion disappears.

The word janapada literally means where a people (jana) first set its feet — which is the whole change in one phrase. Between roughly 1000 and 600 BCE people came to be connected not only by kinship but by belonging to a land, and land gave them a far more concrete identity. Control over land, over agricultural production and over trade routes now mattered, and administration grew more complex to match.

From about 600 BCE to 300 CE came the mahājanapadas — bigger units than janapadas. Sources usually speak of sixteen. Among them Magadha, in present-day Bihar, became the most powerful: strategic location, fertile plains and strong rulers let it expand over its neighbours, and that expansion became the Mauryan empire. Alongside monarchies (rājyas) there were republics — gaṇas or saṁghas.

Bharata
The name Bharata first appears in the Ṛigveda, used of the Bharata jana — the people ruled by the family of the Bharatas. A people named after the family that led them: that is what kin-based political identity looks like from the inside.
05Three assembliesSabhā, samiti and vidhata30 XP

The rājā did not decide alone. The Ṛigveda and the Atharvaveda name three assemblies — sort each description to the right one.

During the early Vedic period the rājā functioned primarily as a clan chief: he led the group in warfare and ensured the protection of its members. He was not an absolute ruler, and each of these three assemblies had a real part in the polity.

Think about it
In modern democracies heads of government exercise power but are bound by constitutions and parliaments — representative bodies that check an elected leader. Do the rājā and these assemblies suggest the same principle? Can we say that governance in India, since its earliest institutionalised forms, was guided by the idea of people taking part in the political process?

In the Deccan the Mauryan empire was succeeded by the Satavāhana empire, which lasted nearly 500 years, from the 2nd century BCE to the 3rd century CE. Further south were the Cholas in the lower Kaveri valley, the Pandyas in the Tamraparni and Vaigai valleys, the Keralaputras — identified with the Cheras in Kerala — and the Satiyaputras in northern Tamil Nadu. All of them are named in Aśhoka's inscriptions.

Much of what we know of these early Tamil kingdoms comes from Sangam literature, the earliest Tamil literary tradition, dating between 300 BCE and 300 CE. The Chera, Chola and Pandya rulers were the three crowned kings, the Vendar of Tamilakam, and their kingdoms flourished in fertile rice-growing regions. These early kingdoms should not be confused with the later imperial Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas, who emerged many centuries afterwards.

The state, for Kauṭilya, is an organic whole: it functions only if all seven constituents stay properly integrated. Remove the treasury and the army stops; remove the territory and there is nothing to defend. That is the force of the line in the Arthaśhāstra — "one wheel does not move the carriage".

The Arthaśhāstra is one of the earliest and most systematic treatises on statecraft anywhere. It gives a detailed picture of administration and insists on training a king in the art of governance. Effective government, on its account, needs cooperation and a well-organised administrative machinery — not a talented individual.

One rock, three dynasties
The Junagadh rock inscription near Girnar in Gujarat carries three rulers from three different periods. Aśhoka's Major Rock Edicts in Prakrit came first, in the 3rd century BCE, spreading the message of Dhamma. The Western Satrap ruler Rudradaman I added a long Sanskrit inscription around 150 CE about his military achievements and the repair of Sudarshana Lake. The Gupta emperor Skandagupta engraved a third record in the 5th century CE, noting the lake's restoration after severe damage. One site, about 700 years. Why would a ruler choose a rock that was already written on?
08What was a king for?The Yajurveda, the Mahābhārata and the Arthaśhāstra30 XP

From the 6th century BCE onwards rulers were known by different titles across different regions — rājā, mahārāja, samrāṭ — reflecting regional political traditions. The Śhānti Parva of the Mahābhārata guides rulers on ethical conduct, justice and responsibility towards subjects. The oath of coronation in the Yajurveda tells the king to judge strong and weak impartially and fairly, to protect the country from all calamities, and to do good to the people.

Punishments for serious offences — the killing of a cow, acts of treachery, the consumption of intoxicating liquor — were severe, sometimes capital. And while kingship was generally hereditary, there are references to kings being elected, and even expelled: royal authority was not always absolute, and succession was not entirely hereditary in practice.

The pan-Indian ambition
Early Indian monarchs did not think only about their own borders. Terms like Jambudvīpa, Bhāratavarṣha, the aśhvamedha and rājasūya yajñas, prithivi and chakravarti kśhetra all carry the idea of authority over the whole subcontinent. The Arthaśhāstra defines prithivi as "the area lying between the Himavat and the sea" and equates it with the chakravarti kśhetra, the domain of a universal paramount ruler. Aśhoka speaks of his exertions changing spiritual life "in Jambudvīpa". A Chera king, Nedunjeral Adan, is described as carrying his conquests to the Himalayas. And in the 11th century the Chola Rajendra I took the title Gangaikonda for his conquests along the Ganga.
09Northern word, southern wordThe same ladder, two vocabularies30 XP

Every large kingdom broke itself into provinces, divisions, districts and villages. The records in the north and in the south use different words for them. Sort these eight.

Between roughly 300 and 800 CE power was steadily decentralised. In the north a kingdom (rājya) was divided into bhuktis, then viṣhayas or bhogas, then adhiṣhṭhānas or paṭṭanas, then vithis — groups of villages, roughly a modern tahsīl — and finally villages. In the south the equivalents are manḍalas or manḍalams, koṭṭams or valanāḍus, nāḍus, and paṭṭalās and kūṛrams. The Chola records use some of these at slightly different levels: manḍalam (province), valanāḍu (district), nāḍu (a group of villages) and finally the ur, the individual village.

Earlier the Satavāhana empire had used āḥāras — administrative divisions, each with its own amātya — with villages below them under a headman, the grāmika. District governors, or pradeśhikas, handled judicial and administrative work, and often decided only after consulting important residents: bankers, caravan leaders, artisans and scribes. The Damodarpur copper plates of Kumaragupta I show a Gupta district office of exactly five members — the head district officer, the chief banker, the chief caravan trader, the chief artisan, and the chief of revenue collection.

Land given away
The Pallavas (about 275–897 CE) made tax-free land grants to villages, known as Brahmadeya villages. The Chālukyas of Badami (about 543–753 CE) granted Brahmin settlements called agrahārams instead — and some of those, at Aihole and Badami, grew into prominent centres of learning. The Gurjara-Pratihāras, Pālas and Rāṣhṭrakūṭas all ran the same mix of monarchy and decentralised units; their three-way contest for Kannauj is known as the tripartite struggle.

The Uttaramerur inscription of Parantaka I, from the 10th century, is carved in the Vaikunṭha Perumal temple in Kanchipuram district, Tamil Nadu. It describes the Kudavolai — the ballot-pot system. Eligible candidates' names were written on palm leaves and placed in a large pot. At a public gathering a young child drew the leaves out one by one to select the members of the village assembly and its committees. The draw took place in full view of the community, often at a temple, so that nobody could claim it had been fixed.

The elected members were divided into specialised committees, or variyams, each with real responsibilities: managing irrigation — the tank committee — administering justice, and collecting taxes, along with gardens and temple management. Village assemblies across early India handled revenue collection, land management, irrigation, road construction and record keeping, and they did it as independent, self-reliant institutions rather than as dependants of the government.

Who was allowed to stand
The same inscription sets conditions on candidates: they should have honest earnings and be pure of mind. A qualification about character, written into the rules of an election, a thousand years ago.

In practice

Early Vedic texts do not indicate a fixed social status strictly based on birth. Social identity was shaped by several overlapping factors — ethnicity, subgroup, region, village, gotra, language, and above all occupation. Occupations remained flexible and mobile; the idea of strictly hereditary occupation had not yet hardened. The earliest reference to the four categories is in the Puruṣhasūkta, a hymn in Book 10 of the Ṛigveda.

The division was a functional one, resting on a system of values in which knowledge came first, then political power, then wealth. The Buddhist Sutta Nipāta puts it flatly: "No brāhmaṇa is such by birth. No outcaste is such by birth but only by his deeds. A brāhmaṇa is such by his deeds." Over time this flexibility gradually reduced.

The same thing, in Tamil
Sangam literature such as the Tolkappiyam also refers to a four-fold division, naming groups like Arasar (kings), Vanigar (traders and merchants) and Velar (farmers), along with Antanar (brāhmaṇas), often patronised by rulers. But its classification too seems premised on occupation, and marked by fluidity. The Pattinappalai describes a prosperous, trade-oriented society in which merchants, salt-makers, fishermen, artisans, agriculturists and warriors all interacted — a society organised by occupation and economic function rather than by rigid varṇa.
12Varṇa, jāti, or both?Two words that are not synonyms30 XP

Jāti emerged gradually and for several reasons: intermarriage among the varṇas, migrating communities becoming endogamous — marrying within their own group — and territorial difference. The number of varṇas was fixed at four; there was never any restriction on the number of jātis, and as new social groups and occupations appeared, the number kept growing.

Neither was as rigid as it is sometimes made to sound. Rulers came from many social backgrounds — the Nandas, Mauryas, Śhuṅgas, Satavāhanas, Vākāṭakas, Guptas and Puṣhyabhūtis among them. The Mandsaur stone inscription of 473 CE records a guild of silk weavers who moved from Lata in Gujarat to Dashapura in Madhya Pradesh and who were proficient not just in weaving but in archery and astrology. The Karitalai copper plates of Mahārāja Jayanātha, late 5th century CE, record brāhmaṇas working as land managers.

Everyone was expected to perform the duties appropriate to their varṇa and to their stage of life. In principle this division gives a person fulfilment in their social, moral and spiritual aspects, and so leads to harmony and balance in society. Every stage came with rituals and ceremonies, particularly the ṣhoḍaśha saṁskāras — the sixteen rites of passage.

Alongside the four stages run the four puruṣhārthas, the goals of life: dharma (righteousness), artha (material well-being), kāma (fulfilment of desires) and mokṣha (liberation from all worldly ties). Dharma and mokṣha carry particular ethical weight, because they give the other two direction: the pursuit of wealth is a desirable objective provided it is guided by dharma, that is, aligned with the welfare of society.

Renouncers
Around the middle of the first millennium BCE a clear trend of renunciants appears — people who advocated non-attachment to material comforts and social relationships and lived as ascetics and wanderers rather than as householders. Sources call them parivrājaka (wanderer), bhikṣhu (one who lives by begging alms) or śhramaṇa (one who strives). Gautama Buddha and Mahāvīra were the most influential thinkers of this trend — but the idea of renunciation was already in the Vedic tradition, in the last two āśhramas.
14Named womenWhat the record actually records30 XP

Pick everything the chapter reports about women in early India. Two of these it flatly contradicts.

The Vedic period is often described as one in which women held a high and respected position. Women took part in scholarly learning and, in some contexts, performed rituals alongside men. Goddesses such as Uṣha, the goddess of dawn, and Aditi, the mother of the gods, held revered positions. The Manu-smṛiti says: "Where women, verily, are honoured, there gods rejoice; where, however, they are not honoured, there all sacred rites prove fruitless."

The chapter is equally plain that this did not simply hold. Over time the position and roles of women fluctuated, and even declined, as social and political conditions changed — while women went on contributing to household management, agriculture, crafts and religious practice throughout. Sangam literature shows women planting, weeding, husking and winnowing paddy, rearing cattle, spinning, fishing, making salt and selling garlands.

Part 5 · What paid for all of it

Agriculture was the backbone. The Mauryan state divided village land into individual holdings while keeping common pasture for grazing, encouraged expansion by clearing forest — though certain forests were protected by law — and taxed land at a fixed proportion of the produce, generally one-sixth. Kauṭilya classifies village land in detail: cultivated, waste or fallow, high and dry, sown, and groves. The Milindapañho describes eight stages of agricultural operations, from preparing and weeding the fields to harvesting and winnowing.

Irrigation mattered enough to be recorded on stone. The Junagadh inscription of Rudradaman I says that Puṣhyagupta, a governor appointed by Chandragupta Maurya, built a dam on Sudarshana Lake near Girnar. The Cholas built canals, lakes and tanks with connecting channels, and the Grand Anicut (Kallanai) of Karikala Chola was repaired by later rulers over many centuries and is in use to this day.

The Nāśhik endowment
The Nāśhik cave inscription of the 2nd century CE records Uṣhavadāta, son-in-law of the Śhaka king Nahapāna, giving a cave to the Saṁgha along with a perpetual endowment of 3,000 kāhāpaṇas — 2,000 invested in one weavers' guild at one pratika a month per hundred, and 1,000 in another at three quarters of a paḍika. The interest maintained the monastery. Two different guilds, two different rates, written into stone: guilds were not just associations of craftspeople but banking and credit institutions that could invest funds securely and generate a steady income.

Part 1 · Making sound, and carrying it
01Pluck the rubber bandActivity 10.1 · sound comes from vibration35 XP

Stretch a rubber band across an open box and pluck it. Something happens that you can both see and hear — and when one stops, so does the other.

Vibrations

Pluck the band and watch it. Then stop it with a finger and see what happens to the sound.

Vibration is the periodic to-and-fro motion of an object. As long as the band vibrates there is sound; the instant it stops, the sound stops with it. The vibrating object is called the source of the sound.

Your own source
Touch your throat gently while you speak. The vibration you feel is in the vocal cords — tightly stretched flaps inside the larynx. Tongue, lips and nasal cavity then shape those vibrations into speech. Grasshoppers and crickets do it differently again, by rubbing their wings or legs together.

The jar is full of air and the bell is easy to hear. Pump the air out and keep watching the hammer.

The bell never stops ringing — you can see the hammer striking throughout. What disappears is the medium. Sound cannot propagate through a vacuum, because there are no particles to pass the disturbance along. Let the air back in and the sound returns.

Which is why spacewalks are silent
Two astronauts repairing a station arm are in a near vacuum. They cannot hear each other speak, and they cannot hear the metal clanging under their own tools. They talk through radios built into their suits — radio waves need no medium at all.
03Solids, liquids, gasesActivities 10.3 and 10.4 · what carries sound35 XP

Press your ear to a desk while a friend scratches the far end. Tap two spoons together underwater. Both times the sound arrives — so air is not the only thing that will carry it.

Sound travels through solids, liquids and gases, and fastest where the particles are packed most closely: roughly 5000 m s⁻¹ in steel, 1500 m s⁻¹ in water and 340 m s⁻¹ in air. Through a vacuum it does not travel at all.

Part 2 · What a sound wave actually is
04Compressions and rarefactionsFig. 10.9 · the piston in the tube50 XP

A piston oscillates at the end of a long tube of air. Push, and the particles near it bunch up. Pull, and they spread apart. Watch what travels down the tube — and, more importantly, what does not.

Travelling
nothing
One particle

With the piston still, the air has a uniform average density all along the tube. Start it oscillating and watch the pattern.

A region of higher-than-average density is a compression (C); a region of lower-than-average density is a rarefaction (R). The alternating series of them travelling outward is a sound wave. The particles themselves go nowhere — each one only oscillates about its own mean position while the disturbance moves on.

What actually reaches your ear
Not the air that was next to the tuning fork. What travels is the energy, handed from particle to particle by collisions. Track the red particle and you will see it shuffling back and forth on the spot while the pattern sweeps past it.
05Longitudinal, and mechanicalFigs. 10.12 and 10.13 · classifying the wave30 XP

In a sound wave the particles vibrate parallel to the direction the wave travels. That makes it a longitudinal wave. In a transverse wave — a ripple on a string, or light — the particles move at right angles to the travel direction instead.

Pick one and watch the arrow showing how the particles move against the arrow showing where the wave goes.

Tick every statement that is true of a sound wave.

A wave that needs a material medium is a mechanical wave, and sound is one. Light is not — which is why sunlight crosses empty space to reach us but the Sun's roar never does.

06Drawing the waveFig. 10.16 · density against distance40 XP

Plot the density of the medium against distance and the bunched-up picture becomes a curve. The peaks sit exactly over the compressions and the dips over the rarefactions.

Above, the particles. Below, the same information as a graph. Label them and see how the two line up.

The highest point of the curve is a crest — maximum density, the middle of a compression. The lowest point is a trough — minimum density, the middle of a rarefaction. The dashed line across the middle is the average density of the undisturbed medium.

Part 3 · Describing the wave
07Wavelength, frequency, time periodSection 10.6.1 · and ν = 1 ÷ T45 XP

Three quantities describe any wave, and two of them are simply two ways of saying the same thing. Drive the sliders and watch which readings move together.

Wavelength is the distance between two consecutive crests. Frequency is how many density oscillations pass a fixed point each second.

Frequency ν is measured in hertz (Hz), one oscillation per second. Time period T is the time for one complete oscillation, in seconds. They are reciprocals: ν = 1 ⁄ T. A shorter time period always means a higher frequency.

08Grains on a drumActivity 10.6 · amplitude, energy and loudness35 XP

Nothing touches the sheet. Turn the amplitude up and watch how high the grains go.

Amplitude is the maximum change in density compared with the average. A larger amplitude carries more energy, so the sheet is pushed further and the grains fly higher — and we perceive it as louder. The energy passing through unit area per unit time is the intensity.

Loudness is not quite intensity
Intensity is a measurable physical quantity. Loudness is what a particular listener perceives, and depends on their hearing. Two people at the same distance from the same speaker receive identical intensity and may honestly report different loudness.

Sound travels fastest where particles are closest together. Switch media and watch the wavelength, not the frequency.

Since v = λ × ν and the frequency is fixed by the source, a faster medium stretches the wavelength. Sound is roughly 4–5 times faster in water than in air, and 15–20 times faster in solids. Warmer, more humid air is also faster: about 331 m s⁻¹ at 0 °C but 344 m s⁻¹ at 22 °C.

Part 4 · How we hear it
11The audible rangeSection 10.6.4 · infrasonic, audible, ultrasonic40 XP

Humans hear from about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz. Sweep the frequency across that band and beyond it, and see who else is listening.

Slide across the whole range. The two boundaries at 20 Hz and 20 kHz are where human hearing stops.

Below 20 Hz is infrasonic — elephants use it, and it is how distant storms and earthquakes are detected. Above 20 kHz is ultrasonic — dogs, cats, bats and dolphins hear it, and it is used for imaging, cleaning and flaw detection. Pitch is how we perceive frequency: high frequency is shrill, low is deep.

Decibels, and when sound becomes noise
Loudness is measured in decibels: rustling leaves a few dB, ordinary conversation about 60 dB, firecrackers over 100 dB. Because the scale is compressed, even a small rise in dB is a large rise in intensity. Unwanted sound is noise, and long exposure above recommended limits damages hearing permanently.
Part 5 · Sound coming back
13EchoSection 10.7.1 · why you need 17 metres45 XP

Clap facing a wall. The sound travels there and back, and if it returns late enough your brain hears it as a separate sound. Move the wall and find the boundary.

Round trip
34 m
Time back
0.10 s
You hear
an echo
Sound travels at about 340 m s⁻¹. Work out how far it gets in the 0.1 s your ear needs to separate two sounds.

Two sounds are heard separately only if they arrive at least 0.1 s apart. In that time sound covers 340 × 0.1 = 34 m — but that is there and back, so the reflecting surface must be at least 17 m away. Closer than that and the reflection merges with the original.

14Echo or reverberation?Section 10.7.2 · one reflection or many30 XP

An echo is a single reflection arriving late enough to be heard as a separate sound — at least 0.1 s. Reverberation is many reflections arriving in a rush, less than 0.05 s apart, so the sound seems to persist after the source has stopped.

Designing a hall for it
Auditoriums are shaped so that reverberation helps rather than blurs — enough for the sound to carry to the back rows, not so much that words run together. Absorbing panels, upholstered seats and heavy curtains soak up the surplus. The Gol Gumbaz at Bijapur does the opposite: its dome returns a whisper several times over.

The pulse goes down and comes back, so the time on the clock covers twice the distance. Halve it before you multiply.

This is echolocation. Bats emit short bursts of ultrasound and read the returning echoes to place obstacles and prey; dolphins and some birds do the same. Humans built sonar on the identical principle, to map the seabed and find submarines and wrecks.

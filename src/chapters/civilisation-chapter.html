What is happening
—

More than 99 per cent of human history falls before writing — roughly three million years ago to five thousand years ago. Everything we know about it comes from artefacts: the tools, implements and other objects people made, used and threw away. It is generally hard to recover what those people thought, and the dating of events and cultures is only approximate.

Less than 1 per cent of human history is the part after writing: the last five thousand years, our own century included. Here both material remains and written documents survive. Literature gives us names, events, and social, political and cultural life, and dating becomes relatively accurate, because documents record the date of a coronation or a war.

Why study any of it?
Because it is the record of two evolutions at once. Biological evolution is the slow physical and genetic change from the australopithecines — australis, southern, and pithecus, primate — to Homo sapiens. Cultural evolution is how humans adapted to the Quaternary Period, the last 26 lakh years, by inventing tools, techniques and technologies to use whatever the climate left them.

One of the earliest known writing systems belongs to the Sindhu–Sarasvatī civilisation, also called the Indus Valley or Harappan civilisation. Writings and engravings on seals and pottery show that the Harappans used a pictographic script. It has not yet been deciphered, so the Sindhu lipi is still unread.

The cuneiform script of the Sumerians in Mesopotamia and the hieroglyphic script of ancient Egypt flourished around the same time, and both have been deciphered. They mark the beginning of the historical period, about 5000 years ago. From about 400 BCE a script called Brahmi was used in parts of southern India and in the Ganga valley, later formalised by the Emperor Aśhoka in the 3rd century BCE and by Iron Age people across southern India.

Let's explore
Look at a Harappan seal and ask why the script resists us. The inscriptions are very short — a handful of signs each — there is no bilingual text like the Rosetta Stone to cross-check against, and we do not know what language is being written. Find out what attempts have been made to decipher it.

Two things move together across the four. The braincase grows, and the face stops jutting forward — by Homo sapiens it sits almost directly under the forehead. The heavy brow ridge that shades Homo erectus is gone by then too.

All of these are hominins: modern humans together with our early human-like ancestors. We know Homo habilis — handy man — from the Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania and Kenya. Its successor Homo erectus, an upright, bipedal ancestor and the inventor of the handaxe and the cleaver, was among the first to migrate out of Africa. Every one of them made tools; only Homo sapiens went on to develop complex technologies.

How we know any of this
Archaeologists dig at early human sites for tools, bones and other objects left behind, and then make and use similar tools themselves to work out how they were held, what they cut and how long they lasted. A fossil is what remains when a body is buried under layers of earth and slowly turns to stone over thousands or millions of years.

Africa, Asia and Europe together hold the earliest human settlements, and are what we call the Old World. Homo erectus was the first hominin to exit Africa, and its handaxes and cleavers turn up across Asia and Europe — which is exactly how we know the tools originated in Africa and spread outwards, between 2 and 0.5 million years ago.

A second major movement out of Africa took place around 125,000 years ago. That one was ours: early Homo sapiens, who had evolved in Africa about 300,000 years ago and are now spread over the whole earth. Between 50,000 and 12,000 years ago they reached Australia and the Americas as well.

Think about it
Why leave at all? Consider what the map shows: the Sahara and Arabia were wetter during interglacials, so the deserts that look like a wall today were sometimes a corridor. Climate opened and shut the doors, and food moved with it.

It was around 3.3 million years ago that one of these ancestors made the earliest stone tools — the moment usually taken as the start of human, rather than animal, behaviour, since no animal has the cognitive ability to make one. That is why human beings came to be called hominins, or tool makers.

Extra-corporal limbs
Tools are sometimes called extra-corporal limbs — limbs outside the body. They worked as extensions of the human body, letting people do things their hands alone could not. People made them, used them, and discarded them when they wore out; the discarded ones were buried in the upper layers of the earth, which is where archaeologists find them.
Part 3 · The ages named after their tools
07Six ages, in orderFig. 4.7 · periods in early human history30 XP

Early human history is divided by technology, not by kings. Tap them oldest first.

Palaeolithic — hunting and gathering, with simple stone tools. Mesolithic — microlithic, tiny stone tools; a transitional phase between hunting–gathering and agriculture. Neolithic — the shift to agriculture and settled life, with domesticated animals and polished stone tools. Chalcolithic — copper used alongside stone, and the first metallurgy. Bronze Age — copper mixed with tin, and with it trade, towns and the early civilisations. Iron Age — iron smelting, stronger tools and weapons, more advanced societies.

Think about it
The move to farming is called the Neolithic Revolution rather than the Neolithic change. Why a revolution, when it took thousands of years? Because of what it overturned: what people ate, where they slept, how many of them there could be, and who owned what.

In the Indian subcontinent the oldest human settlement dates back about 2 million years. Attirampakkam in Tamil Nadu is dated to about 1.5–1.7 million years ago and Isampur in Karnataka to 1.2 million years ago. Animal fossils and large cutting tools — handaxes and cleavers — were found there, along with scrapers and choppers of quartzite and limestone, used to chop meat, dig out tubers, scrape skin and cleave bone for the protein-rich marrow inside.

Later came the bow and arrow, and parallel-sided blade and microblade tools made from glassy rock, which is very sharp when freshly struck. With them came symbolic communication: painted cave and rock-shelter walls, pigment on the body, and the first beads of stone, bone and shell. A burin, or engraver, was used to cut symbols into bone and shell such as ostrich eggs, and microblades were fixed into a handle to be used.

Bhimbetka
Around 12,000 years ago the climate warmed. Forests and grasslands spread into land that had been under ice, offering small game, fish and edible wild grains — and the world saw its first population explosion. Fishing became the mainstay. The World Heritage Site of Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh has hundreds of painted rock shelters with Mesolithic and earlier occupation.

As hunter–gatherers grew familiar with the seasons and with what each one offered, there was a gradual shift to producing food instead of finding it. The hallmark of the revolution was domestication: selected animals and plants brought under human control, and new breeds developed by cultivation and husbandry.

Hunter–gatherers made tools to procure food. Neolithic farmers made tools to produce and process it, and developed earthenware pottery in a variety of shapes and sizes. They drew on many raw materials and established the first village settlements — which is the foundation the urban revolution was later built on.

Not everywhere at once
Fig. 4.14 makes one point above all others: this transition did not happen at the same time everywhere. Each region reached each stage in its own century, with its own crop — millets in India, rice on the Ganga plains, pearl millet in West Africa, wheat and barley in West Asia, millets in north China and rice on the Yangtze.
11Mehrgarh, 7000 BCEFig. 4.16 · the oldest Neolithic site30 XP

Pick everything the chapter actually reports from Mehrgarh. Some of these belong to other sites entirely.

In the northwest, Mehrgarh on the Bolan river — in present-day Pakistan — is the oldest Neolithic site and the earliest agricultural village in the subcontinent, dating to about 7000 BCE. Its people built handmade sun-dried brick houses and granaries, buried their dead in graves, and made ornaments from semi-precious stones such as lapis lazuli and carnelian, and from shell.

They cultivated wheat and barley and raised sheep, goats and Indian cattle — the zebu humped bull in particular. They were also the first to make copper objects, entering the metal age and becoming Chalcolithic people by about 4000 BCE. That is the foundation the Bronze Age Sindhu–Sarasvatī civilisation was built on. By 2500 BCE most of the subcontinent was occupied by Neolithic farming communities, herding cattle, sheep and goats and growing cereals, millets and pulses.

Sanganakallu
Not every Neolithic site sits on a river plain. Sanganakallu in Karnataka, dating to about 2000 BCE, sits on top of a granite hill — and in parts of the country these Neolithic communities were living alongside, and trading with, contemporary Chalcolithic cultures.

The Harappans had their own standard systems of weight and measurement — which is what their meticulously planned settlements and their efficient interregional trade both depended on. For small units they followed a binary multiple: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and so on. For larger denominations they used multiples of ten. Cubical stone weights of exactly this kind have been reported from several sites.

Think about it
Every whole number up to 31 can be made from 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16, and in exactly one way — try it. Now imagine long-distance trade between Harappa, Lothal and the Persian Gulf if each town had weighed by a different standard. What would a merchant have had to carry, and how would any price have been agreed?
13The Harappan timelineFig. 4.18 · village to city and back30 XP

Six stages, from a Neolithic village to the last Harappan sites. Tap them oldest first.

Copper tools in the fertile alluvial plains of the Indus and the Ghaggar–Sarasvatī raised productivity and prosperity, and with it came large-scale pottery production in strongly regional styles. Those regional styles, visible in the early Chalcolithic sites from 4000 BCE onwards, evolved into the standard features of the civilisation by about 2500 BCE. Some radio-carbon dates put the pre-Harappan phase at sites in the dried Sarasvatī belt — Bhirrana and Kunal — between 7000 and 5500 BCE.

Because these sites show cultural continuity, the stage before the cities is called Early Harappan. Pottery traditions, semi-precious stone beads, shell bangles, terracotta objects and copper working all carry through. So, probably, do the perimeter wall around a settlement, the use of seals, and even the script itself: graffiti on pottery and a few seals with geometric and simple animal motifs from Harappa, Rehman Dheri and Kunal look very much like forerunners of it.

A tradition nearly 5,000 years old
The Early Harappans ploughed the field at Kalibangan in two directions at once — horizontal furrows crossing vertical ones. That is a field carrying two crops in the same year, exactly like the seasonal Rabi and Kharif cultivation practised in India today.

Four early world civilisations emerged in river plains: the Harappan along the Sindhu and Sarasvatī, the Mesopotamian around the Euphrates and Tigris in West Asia, the Egyptian along the Nile, and the Chinese in the Huang He basin of northern China. Mesopotamia and the Indus–Ghaggar-Sarasvatī valleys are geographically close, and that proximity gave them strong contact and trade. Egypt and China show little tangible evidence of direct contact with the Sindhu–Sarasvatī civilisation.

The cuneiform tablets of the Akkadian king Sargon mention trade with Dilmun, Magan and Meluhha. Meluhha is generally identified with the Sindhu–Sarasvatī civilisation; Dilmun and Magan are today's Bahrain and the Oman peninsula. With the Harappans they traded semi-precious stone beads, ivory, timber, gold dust and probably copper.

Don't miss out
While the Sumerians were building canals, the Early Harappans in the Baluchistan foothills were building check dams across small streams, called gabarbands. A far more evolved form of the same skill is the water harvesting system at Dholavira in Kachchh, where two streams were dammed and diverted into a chain of deep tanks — some cut into the bedrock — to hold the water inside the city boundary. The huge dockyard at Lothal, built entirely of burnt bricks, is the other marvel.

Mesopotamia's four city-based civilisations followed one another in turn. The Sumerians came first, at Ur and other cities in southern Mesopotamia — the first to build dams and canals for irrigation, and the first to use mud brick and burnt brick. The Akkadians overshadowed them in 2334 BCE and built the world's first dynastic empire. The Assyrians took supremacy around 2154 BCE from Assur in the north and lasted till the early 1700s BCE. The Babylonians rose in central Mesopotamia from 1900 BCE, and with Hammurabi from 1792 BCE came the Code of Hammurabi, a compilation of rules for civil and social conduct that later legal systems were modelled on.

The ziggurat
Each Sumerian city built a grand stepped temple for its chief god — a ziggurat, a tower-like pyramid of several floors with the main temple on top — and the city grew around it. Agriculture, trade and the transport of goods were all tied to temple authority, entry to the sacred top was restricted to high priests and priestesses who were probably part of the ruling class, and because the ziggurat also served as the city's treasury, the surrounding wall had only one entrance.

Part 1 · Around the edge
01A walk around the borderSection 6.1 · Figs. 6.2A and 6.2B40 XP

Imagine a tiny insect setting off along the border of a shape, never turning around, until it arrives back where it started. The distance it walks is the perimeter.

Distance walked
—
Whole way round
—
Which is
—
Pick a shape, then walk the insect all the way round. The distance it ends on is the perimeter.

A square with side a has perimeter 4a; an equilateral triangle with side a has perimeter 3a. A rectangle with length a and width b has perimeter 2(a + b) — and the square's formula is that one with a = b, a special case of it. The L-shape is a reminder that the perimeter is the walk, not a formula you look up: you add the sides you actually have.

02The ratio that refuses to moveSection 6.1 · Fig. 6.440 XP

Double the side of a square and its perimeter doubles too. Whatever size you make it, the perimeter is always four times the side. This turns out to be the whole reason π exists.

Small: P ÷ side
—
Big: P ÷ side
—
The ratio
—
Change the size. The two ratios stay locked together — and locked to the same number.

For every square the ratio of perimeter to side is 4 : 1; for every equilateral triangle it is 3 : 1; for a rectangle that is always twice as long as it is wide, 6 : 1. The number depends on the shape and not at all on the size. So it is fair to ask the same question about a circle — what is the ratio of its perimeter to its width across? That number has a name.

03Roll the wheel and measureSection 6.2 · the C/D ratio50 XP

The book suggests wrapping thread round a cotton reel to measure the ratio. Rolling the circle along a line does the same job: one full turn lays out exactly one circumference.

Diameter D
—
Track laid C
—
C ÷ D
—
Roll wheels of three different sizes. The track length changes every time — the ratio does not.

Whatever the wheel, C ÷ D lands on the same number, a shade over 3.14. In 1706 William Jones gave it the Greek letter π, after perimetros; Euler made the symbol stick. So C = πD, and because the diameter is twice the radius, C = 2πr. The whole of the rest of this chapter is built on those five characters.

Part 2 · Pinning down π
04Trapping π between two polygonsSection 6.2 · Figs. 6.6 and 6.755 XP

Archimedes could not measure a curve, so he refused to try. He put a polygon inside the circle and another outside it, measured both — straight lines are easy — and trapped π between them.

π is more than
—
π is less than
—
Gap left
—
Start at six sides, where the answer is already 3 < π < 3.46. Then keep adding sides.

With a hexagon the inner perimeter is exactly 6r, so π > 3 — which is what Fig. 6.6 is showing you. Archimedes worked by hand all the way to 96 sides and got 310⁄71 < π < 31⁄7, correct to two decimal places, in 250 BCE. Liu Hui and then Zu Chongzhi pushed the same method to 24,576 sides.

05Everyone's best guess, on one lineSection 6.2 · from 1900 BCE to 1400 CE45 XP

Every civilisation that built anything round needed a value for π. Here they are on a number line, magnified until you can see who was closest.

—

Tap each in turn and watch the error shrink by a factor of ten, then a hundred.

Zu Chongzhi's 355⁄113 was the most accurate value anyone had for over 800 years, and no fraction with a denominator under 15,000 gets closer. But notice what Āryabhaṭa wrote alongside his own value: āsanna — approaching. He was saying the ratio could not be written exactly as a fraction at all, and he was right.

06Mādhava's infinite seriesSection 6.2 · the first exact formula50 XP

Every value before this was an approximation someone had measured or squeezed. Mādhava of Saṅgamagrāma, around 1400 CE, wrote down something different: a formula that is exactly π, if you are willing to go on forever.

Estimate of π
—
Off by
—
This term
—
Add terms one at a time. Each one overshoots, then undershoots — and the target is squeezed between them.

π⁄4 = 1 − 1⁄3 + 1⁄5 − 1⁄7 + … Nothing round appears anywhere in it — just the odd numbers, alternately added and subtracted. Moving from cutting circles up to summing numbers is the shift that begins calculus. It converges slowly, which is why later series by Nīlakaṇṭha, Machin, Ramanujan and the Chudnovsky brothers were worth finding; today π is known to trillions of digits.

Part 3 · Arcs, sectors and a running track
07How long is a piece of the rim?Section 6.4 · Figs. 6.8 to 6.1045 XP

Fold a circle in half and the two halves must have equal length, so each semicircle is πr. Quarter it and each piece is πr/2. There is an obvious pattern, and it is the right one.

Fraction of the circle
—
Whole circumference
—
Arc length
—
Find 360°, then 180°, then 90°. Each time the arc is that fraction of the whole way round.

An arc that sweeps θ° at the centre is θ⁄360 of the whole rim, so its length is 2πr × θ⁄360. Put θ = 180 and you get πr, the semicircle; put θ = 90 and you get πr/2, the quarter. Those two are not separate facts to memorise — they are this one formula, read at two settings.

08Why the runners don't start levelSection 6.4 · Figs. 6.1 and 6.1155 XP

The question this chapter opens with. A 400 m track has two straights of 84.39 m and two semicircular ends; the inner semicircle has radius 36.5 m and each lane is 1.22 m wide. Runners keep about 0.3 m off the inner border.

Running radius
—
One lap
—
Stagger from lane 1
—
Walk out through the lanes. The straights never change — only the bends do.

Lane 1 comes to 168.78 + 231.22 = 400 m exactly, which is where the number comes from. Every lane out, the two bends together gain 2π × 1.22 = 7.67 m, and that is the stagger. Notice what it does not depend on: not the length of the straights, and not the radius of the bend. So the same stagger separates lanes 2 and 3 as lanes 1 and 2 — and a smaller 200 m track needs exactly the same stagger, which answers the question in the opening Think and Reflect.

Part 4 · The area of a straight-edged shape
10Lean it over, and nothing is lostSection 6.7 · Figs. 6.17 to 6.1945 XP

Cut the triangle off one end of a parallelogram and carry it round to the other end. You have a rectangle, made of exactly the same material — so the two must have the same area.

Area, b × h
—
Slanting side
—
Perimeter
—
Lean it further and further over. Watch which number moves and which one refuses to.

The area is base × height, and leaning the shape over changes neither. But look at the perimeter while you do it: the slanting sides get longer and longer. So two parallelograms can have the same four side lengths and different areas — which answers the book's Think and Reflect. Knowing the sides of a parallelogram is not enough to know its area, and the same turns out to be true of any 4-gon.

11A median cuts the area in halfSection 6.8 · Fig. 6.2245 XP

Join a vertex to the middle of the opposite side. The two triangles you have made are almost never the same shape as each other — but they always have the same area.

Left half
—
Right half
—
Same shape?
—
Drag the apex right off to one side. The two halves look nothing alike — and still measure the same.

Both halves stand on equal bases, because D is the midpoint, and both reach up to the same apex, so they have the same height too — the height is measured to the line BC, which is one line for both of them. Half base times height is therefore the same number twice. It should feel surprising: the two triangles are genuinely different, and the area cannot tell.

12Heron: area from the three sides aloneSection 6.8.1 · Examples 3 to 555 XP

No height, no angle, no diagram — just three lengths. Heron of Alexandria found that they are enough.

Semi-perimeter s
—
Heron's answer
—
Half base × height
—
Try 3, 4, 5. Then 7, 24, 25. Then try 1, 2, 9 — and see what the formula does when no such triangle exists.

With s = ½(a + b + c), the area is √(s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)). It is a strange-looking thing, and it is worth checking against cases you already know: an equilateral triangle of side a gives √3⁄4a², and 3-4-5 gives 6, which is also ½ × 3 × 4 because that triangle is right-angled. The formula even polices itself: if one side is longer than the other two together, a bracket goes negative and there is no real square root — because there is no such triangle.

Brahmagupta generalises it
In 628 CE Brahmagupta found the same kind of formula for a cyclic 4-gon with sides a, b, c, d: √((s−a)(s−b)(s−c)(s−d)). Now shrink the fourth side to nothing. With d = 0 the 4-gon becomes a triangle, s − d is just s, and Brahmagupta's formula turns into Heron's. Heron's is the special case; Brahmagupta's is the generalisation.
13Work the areasExercise Set 6.2 · End-of-chapter Q2 to Q640 XP

Five triangles, described the way the exercises describe them. Find each area.

Row four is a warning about reaching for Heron too quickly: 7, 24, 25 satisfies 7² + 24² = 25², so it is right-angled and the area is simply ½ × 7 × 24 = 84. Heron gives 84 as well, with a great deal more arithmetic. Row five runs the other way — you are given the area and asked for a side, so you work back through ½ × base × height to find the height, then Baudhāyana–Pythagoras for the equal sides.

Part 5 · The area inside a circle
14Cut it into wedges and straighten it outSection 6.10 · Fig. 6.3755 XP

Nīlakaṇṭha Somayājī's picture, from around 1500. Slice the disc into wedges, then lay them out alternately up and down. The more slices, the more the result looks like a parallelogram.

Base becomes
—
Height becomes
—
Area
—
Lay the wedges out, then keep raising the number of slices and watch the top edge flatten.

Half the wedges point up and half point down, so the base of the row is half the circumference, πr, and its height is the radius. Area = base × height = πr². Archimedes had reached the same answer eighteen centuries earlier by a different route — the area of a circle equals the area of a right triangle whose legs are the radius and the circumference, ½ × 2πr × r — but this picture is the one you can see in a single glance.

15Sectors, and the segment left overSection 6.10.1 · Figs. 6.38 to 6.4045 XP

A sector is the slice bounded by two radii and the arc between them. Cut the slice with a straight chord instead and what is left beyond the chord is a segment.

Sector area
—
Triangle inside it
—
Segment left
—
Set 180° and the sector is half the disc. Set 90° and it is a quarter. In between, the same fraction rule.

A sector is θ⁄360 of the disc, so its area is πr² × θ⁄360 — the same fraction argument that gave the arc its length, applied to area instead. The minor segment is what is left when you take the triangle away from the sector, and the triangle's own area is ½r²sin θ. At θ = 60° that gives πr²/6 − √3⁄4r², which is the book's starred question 7 written out.

Part 1 · What kind of mixture is this?
01Three beakers and a laserActivity 5.1 · the test that tells them apart30 XP

Salt in water. Chalk powder in water. A few drops of milk in water. Stirred well, two of them look much alike. Three tests tell them apart.

Run all three tests on the beakers.

A well-stirred mixture of sugar and water is equally sweet in the first and the last sip. Its composition is uniform throughout, and such a mixture is called a homogeneous mixture, or a solution. Vinegar (acetic acid in water) and soda (carbon dioxide in water) are homogeneous too. A solution always remains homogeneous.

A stirred mixture of sand and water is not uniform. The sand particles are easily visible and settle with time. That is a heterogeneous mixture.

But beaker C is the interesting one. It looks uniform, it does not settle, and it passes through the filter paper — yet the laser beam is visible through it. It is not a solution and it is not a suspension. It is a third thing.

Safety first: never look directly into a laser beam. Now — what does a visible beam through beaker C tell you?
The path of the beam was not visible in solution A. In B the suspended chalk scattered the light. In C the light was scattered too, though the mixture looked homogeneous. That scattering of light by particles is the Tyndall effect, named after John Tyndall who first explained it.

The particles of a solute in a solution are the smallest — less than 1 nm across. A colloid has larger particles, 1 to 1000 nm. A suspension has much larger particles still, more than 1000 nm in diameter, large enough to see with the naked eye.

Unlike a suspension, the particles in a colloid do not settle out over time. They stay uniformly spread through the mixture, as in a solution — which is why milk, tomato sauce, ice cream and blood look uniform but are not solutions at all.

Threads of curiosity
Blood can be separated by centrifugation, and blood coagulates — so is it a suspension? But blood cells cannot be seen with the naked eye — so is it a solution? Neither. Blood is a colloid.
03Uniform, or notSorting ten everyday mixtures25 XP

Homogeneous means the same all the way through. Heterogeneous means it is not. Some of these are less obvious than they look.

An alloy is the surprise on this list. When metals are melted at high temperature they can be mixed, and the mixture solidifies into what looks like a single metal. Brass is roughly 80% copper and 20% zinc; bronze is roughly 80% copper and 20% tin; stainless steel is iron with carbon, chromium, nickel and molybdenum. An alloy is a homogeneous mixture, and no physical method will separate it.

Gases are the other surprise. Gas particles move freely in all directions, so they mix easily and uniformly — most gas mixtures are homogeneous, including the hydrogen and oxygen used as rocket fuel. But smoke (solid particles in air), fog (liquid droplets in air) and dust in air are heterogeneous.

Part 2 · How much is dissolved?
05The concentration benchExamples 5.1, 5.2 and 5.335 XP

The amount of solute dissolved in a given amount of solvent or solution is the concentration. There are three ways to write it as a percentage, and which one you use depends on what is easy to measure.

Working
—
Concentration
—
—

Mass by mass (% m/m) tells you how many grams of solute are in 100 grams of solution. It is the usual way to label packaged food, spice mixtures and milk powder.

Mass by volume (% m/v) tells you how many grams of solute are in 100 millilitres of solution. It is used where measuring the volume of a liquid is easier than weighing it — medicines and laboratories.

Volume by volume (% v/v) tells you how many millilitres of solute are in 100 millilitres of solution. It is used when two miscible liquids are mixed: perfumes, cosmetics and vinegar.

Meet a scientist
Dilip Mahalanabis, an Indian paediatrician, first developed and implemented the treatment for dehydration caused by diseases such as diarrhoea and cholera. He formulated the ORS that revolutionised rehydration therapy, and it has saved millions of lives since the World Health Organization popularised it worldwide. ORS works because the proportions are fixed — change the salt, the sugar or the water and it is no longer ORS.
06Which measure would you use?Choosing between m/m, m/v and v/v25 XP

Each of these is labelled one particular way, and there is a reason each time.

A saline drip in a hospital is usually 0.9 % m/v sodium chloride in water — 0.9 g of salt in 100 mL of solution. That strength is safe for blood and replaces lost fluids in the body.

Note
Industry commonly says weight by weight percentage (% w/w), because weight and mass are generally used interchangeably. Numerically % m/m and % w/w are equal.

Why mention the temperature at all? Because the solubility of a solid solute in a liquid solvent generally increases with temperature. Gases dissolved in liquids do the opposite — their solubility generally decreases as temperature rises.

Compare the four curves. Sodium chloride barely moves between 10 °C and 80 °C, from 36 g to 37 g. Potassium nitrate goes from 21 g to 167 g over the same range. That difference is what makes one of them easy to crystallise by cooling and the other not.

A student makes a saturated solution of potassium chloride in water at 80 °C and leaves it to cool to room temperature. What will she observe?
At 80 °C, 100 g of water holds 54 g of potassium chloride. At around 25 °C it holds roughly 35 g. The excess cannot stay in solution, so it separates out as solid — which is exactly the principle the next mission is built on.
Part 3 · Separating homogeneous mixtures

A crystal is a solid made up of particles arranged in a regular geometric pattern. In the laboratory, forming crystals from a saturated solution is called crystallization. It separates two solids when one is present in small quantity and both dissolve in the same solvent, and it purifies solids — because a substance's solubility differs at different temperatures.

Cooling slowly gives the particles in the solution enough time to come together, producing larger, shiny, well-shaped crystals. Cooling rapidly in ice-cold water gives smaller, less well-formed ones.

India's scientific contributions
Crystallization of salt was an ancient process used by coastal communities in India. Panga salt was obtained by boiling concentrated sea brines, while evaporation of seawater produced karkatch salt. The two methods gave salt crystals of different sizes.
10The 25-degree ruleDistillation · Table 5.540 XP

Distillation allows the recovery of the solvent, or the separation of liquids whose boiling points differ by at least about 25 °C. It also separates a liquid from a solution containing a dissolved solid — which evaporation cannot do, because evaporation lets the solvent escape.

Vapours of the lower-boiling liquid pass through a condenser, are cooled by circulating water or air, and are collected as a pure liquid in a separate vessel. Whatever remains stays in the distillation flask. Acetone boils at about 56 °C and water at 100 °C, so a mixture of the two separates cleanly.

When the difference is smaller than 25 °C, simple distillation is not enough and fractional distillation is used instead. A petroleum refinery works this way, splitting crude oil into petroleum gas, petrol, kerosene, diesel, lubricating oil and bitumen. The gaseous fraction is compressed into steel cylinders, where it liquefies — that is LPG.

India's scientific contributions
In Kannauj, Uttar Pradesh — the perfume capital of India — the earthy smell that rises after the first rain is captured as a natural perfume called Mitti ka Ittar, using a traditional distillation method known as the Deg-Bhapka method, passed down through generations.
11Writing with colourActivity 5.5 · paper chromatography30 XP

A pencil line 2 cm from the bottom of a paper strip, a spot of ink on it, and the lower end dipped in water — with the water level below the spot. Then wait.

—

As the water rises through the paper, the ink separates into different coloured spots. This is paper chromatography. It uses the differences in how the components interact with the solvent and with the paper: the liquid carries the substances up the paper, separating them by how fast each one moves.

The same method separates the pigments in a green spinach extract, or the colours in flower petals. Water will not work as the solvent in every case — sometimes you need alcohol, or a mixture of solvents. For green food colour, a 2 % m/v salt solution works well.

Threads of curiosity
'Chromatography' comes from the Greek chroma, colour, and graphein, to write — literally 'writing with colour', because it was first used to separate coloured substances like dyes and inks.
Part 4 · Separating heterogeneous mixtures
12Two layers, one tapActivity 5.6 · the separating funnel30 XP

5 mL of mustard oil and 20 mL of water, poured into a separating funnel and left to stand. Open the stopcock in the right order and you get both liquids back.

Two liquids that do not mix are immiscible. Drain in the right order.

Oil and water form separate layers because they are immiscible, and the denser liquid sits below. Mustard oil is less dense than water, so the yellow oil forms the upper layer and water the lower one. Open the stopcock slowly, collect the water, close it when the water is almost fully drained, discard the small portion that contains both, and then collect the oil separately.

A litre of cooking oil is labelled 910 g, while a litre of water is about 1000 g. That is the whole reason the oil floats.

13Solid straight to vapourActivity 5.7 · sublimation30 XP

Crushed camphor mixed with sand, in a china dish, with an inverted funnel over it and its nozzle plugged with cotton. Light the burner and watch the inner wall of the funnel.

—

Camphor, heated below its melting point, changes directly from solid to vapour without passing through the liquid state. That is sublimation. On cooling, the vapour condenses straight back to a solid without becoming a liquid, which is deposition. Camphor sublimes and separates from the sand, because sand does not sublime on heating.

Solid carbon dioxide — dry ice, used for storing ice cream — also sublimes. So does naphthalene, which can replace camphor in this activity.

14Clearing the muddy waterSedimentation · coagulation · centrifugation35 XP

Powdered alum (fitkari) is a white crystalline substance often used in water purification. It makes the fine suspended particles clump together — this is coagulation, and the alum is the coagulant. The larger clumps then settle by gravity, which is sedimentation, and can be separated by decantation or filtration.

Centrifugation spins a mixture in a tube at high speed. The tubes swing horizontal and the centrifugal force drives the heavier particles outwards, so they settle at the bottom while the lighter liquid stays on top. It is how a blood sample is separated into plasma, platelets and white cells, and red blood cells.

Bridging science and society
A paperfuge is a hand-powered device built from a cardboard disc, string and a handle — the same idea as a spinning toy. It spins blood samples fast enough to separate the heavier components from the lighter ones with no electricity at all, which helps detect diseases like malaria and anaemia in remote areas.
Threads of curiosity
Making cheese (paneer) from milk is coagulation too — lemon juice or vinegar acts as the coagulant, clumping the milk proteins together.
15Pick the right techniqueRevise, Reflect, Refine · Table 5.340 XP

Seven mixtures. Every one of them has a property you can exploit — size, density, solubility, boiling point or the ability to sublime.

You are given one mixture of sand, common salt and naphthalene together. In what order would you separate all three?
Naphthalene sublimes and the other two do not, so heating removes it cleanly first. Of what remains, salt dissolves in water and sand does not — so filtration leaves the sand on the paper and the salt in the filtrate. Evaporating or crystallising the filtrate recovers the salt.

Separation happens outside the laboratory too. Your kidneys remove waste from your blood. Sewage treatment uses sedimentation, coagulation and filtration before water is released, and the water recovered can be reused for flushing or watering plants. Sorting waste at home is the same idea — dry waste like plastic, paper, glass and metal can be recycled, wet waste can be composted, and researchers are working on recovering lithium from old phone and laptop batteries.

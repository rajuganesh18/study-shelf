Part 1 · What a circle is
01Everything at one distanceSection 5.1 · the definition40 XP

Humans noticed circles in raindrops, tree rings and the moon long before anyone defined one. The definition turns the observation around: a circle is the set of all points on a plane at a given distance from a given point. Move a point about and watch which side of that definition it falls on.

5 away
Which puts it
on the circle
Find the inside, the outside, and the circle itself. Direction never decides it — only distance does.

Notice that the direction slider changes nothing about the verdict. That is the whole content of the definition: every point at exactly the radius is on the circle, whichever way it lies from the centre, and no point at any other distance is. The set of points satisfying a condition like this is called a locus.

Five words: centre, radius, chord, diameter, arc. Tap each in turn.

Two of these catch people out. A diameter is not merely the longest distance across — it is a chord that passes through the centre, which is why it is the longest chord there is. And an arc is a piece of the circle itself, the curved part, not the straight line joining its ends.

03Turn it, fold it, nothing changesSection 5.2 · symmetries45 XP

Look at a wheel touching the ground. Look again later. Is it the same point of the wheel touching down? There is no way to tell — and that is exactly what makes a circle special.

After rotating
—
The fold line
—
Passes through O?
—
Rotate through any angle at all, then fold. Both leave the circle exactly where it was.

A circle has complete rotational symmetry — turn it by any angle about its centre and it lands on itself. It also has a line of reflection symmetry through every diameter, and infinitely many of them. That second fact is Amina's trick for finding a lost centre: fold the paper circle twice so the boundary meets itself, and the two creases cross at the centre, because every fold line is a diameter.

The longest chord, and the shortest
In a circle of radius 5 the longest chord is the diameter, 10 units. There is no shortest chord: push a chord away from the centre and it keeps shrinking, right down to a single point where it meets the circle — but a point is not a chord, so no smallest one exists.
Part 2 · How many circles?
04Circles through two pointsSection 5.3 · Fig. 5.450 XP

Fix two points A and B. How many circles pass through both? Slide the centre and count.

Every centre on the dashed line gives a circle through both points. Keep several.

Any point equidistant from A and B can be the centre, and the set of such points is exactly the perpendicular bisector of AB. So there are infinitely many circles through two points, and their centres all lie on that one line. The smallest is the one with AB as a diameter — radius half of AB — and moving away from AB in either direction makes the circle larger and its arc through A and B flatter.

05Three points, exactly one circleTheorem 1 · Figs. 5.5 to 5.755 XP

Find an acute triangle, a right-angled one, and an obtuse one. Then try putting C on the line AB.

Because OA = OB, the centre must lie on the perpendicular bisector of AB; because OA = OC, it must lie on the bisector of AC as well. Two lines that are not parallel meet at exactly one point, so there is exactly one circumcentre and exactly one circumcircle. Put C on the line through A and B and the two bisectors become parallel, they never meet, and no circle passes through all three.

06Where does the centre sit?Section 5.3 · Figs. 5.5 to 5.735 XP

For each triangle, say where its circumcentre lies.

The right-angled case is the memorable one: the circumcentre lands exactly on the midpoint of the hypotenuse. That is the same fact as the corollary later in this chapter — the angle in a semicircle is 90° — seen from the other end.

Part 3 · Chords, angles and distances
07Equal chords, equal anglesTheorems 2 and 3 · Figs. 5.9 to 5.1145 XP

Tie a thread between two points on a wheel and pull it tight — that is a chord. Rotate the wheel and the thread becomes a different chord of the same length. Does the angle it makes at the centre change?

Verdict

Make the two angles equal, then unequal. Watch what the lengths do — and where the chords sit never matters.

Both directions are true, and both are proved by triangle congruence. Theorem 2: equal chords subtend equal angles at the centre — the two triangles have three matching sides (two radii and the chord), so they are congruent by SSS and the angles between the radii match. Theorem 3 is the converse: equal angles at the centre give equal chords, this time by SAS.

08Straight to the midpointTheorems 4 and 5 · Fig. 5.1250 XP

Join the centre to the middle of a chord. The segment you have drawn is always at right angles to the chord — no matter which chord you pick.

Angle at M
90°
AM and MB

Move the chord anywhere at all. The angle at M does not budge off 90°.

Triangle OAB is isosceles, because OA and OB are both radii. M is the midpoint, so AM = MB, and triangles OMA and OMB are congruent by SSS. That makes ∠OMA = ∠OMB — and since they sit on a straight line and add to 180°, each must be 90°. Read backwards (Theorem 5) it says the perpendicular dropped from the centre to a chord always bisects it.

Set r = 13 and d = 5. Then push the chord out to the very edge, and pull it right onto the centre.

The radius, the distance and half the chord form a right-angled triangle, so d² + (half-chord)² = r², giving the chord length as 2√(r² − d²). That single formula carries three of the chapter's theorems: chords the same distance out are the same length (Theorems 6 and 7), and pushing a chord further out makes it shorter (Theorem 8). At d = 0 the chord is a diameter, the longest there is; at d = r it has shrunk to a single point.

10Work the chord numbersExercise Set 5.5 · End-of-chapter Q1, Q3, Q4, Q940 XP

Each row gives you two of the three quantities. Find the third.

Every one is the same right triangle read a different way round: r² = d² + (chord/2)². Knowing any two of r, d and the chord gives you the third, and it is worth being able to run it in all three directions rather than memorising three formulas.

Part 4 · Arcs and the angles they subtend
11Major arc, minor arcSection 5.7 · Figs. 5.17 and 5.1840 XP

Two points on a circle cut it into two arcs. There are always two ways round, and the chapter needs you to be clear about which one you mean.

This arc sweeps
—
Which makes it
—
The other one
—
Follow each arc in turn, and find the setting where neither is bigger than the other.

The angle subtended by an arc at the centre is the angle you sweep as you travel along that arc from one end to the other. The minor arc always sweeps less than 180° and the major arc more, and the two together always come to 360°. When the two points are exactly opposite the chord is a diameter, both arcs sweep 180°, and neither is major nor minor — they are semicircles.

12The centre sees doubleTheorem 9 · Figs. 5.20 to 5.2355 XP

Fix an arc. Look at the angle it makes at the centre, and the angle it makes at a point out on the circle. Then move that point anywhere you like along the far side.

Ratio

Slide P right along the arc. The angle at the centre is fixed — but so is the angle at P.

This is the most surprising result in the chapter. The angle an arc subtends at the centre is exactly twice the angle it subtends at any point on the circle outside that arc — and because the centre's angle does not move, neither does the point's, wherever on the far arc you put it. Every point out there sees the arc at the same angle. That single fact is what the rest of the chapter is built on.

13The angle in a semicircleCorollary · Fig. 5.2445 XP

Move D anywhere along the upper half. The angle at D refuses to change.

The arc from A to B not containing D is half the circle, so it subtends a straight angle of 180° at the centre. By the theorem before, the angle at D is half of that: 90°, always. A corollary is a fact that follows immediately from a result already proved, and this is the chapter's cleanest example of one.

Part 5 · Four points on one circle
14Opposite angles of a cyclic 4-gonTheorems 10 and 11 · Fig. 5.2850 XP

Put four points on a circle and join them up in order. The quadrilateral you get is called cyclic, and its angles are not free to be anything they like.

∠A + ∠C
—
∠B + ∠D
—
Each pair
—
Move the vertices anywhere on the circle. Both sums stay pinned at 180°.

∠A is half the angle that arc BCD subtends at the centre, and ∠C is half the angle that arc BAD subtends. Between them those two arcs make the whole circle, 360°, so the two angles come to half of that: 180°. The converse is Theorem 12 — if two opposite angles of a quadrilateral add to 180°, its four vertices must lie on a circle.

15Find the missing angleEnd-of-chapter Q2, Q6, Q7, Q840 XP

The fourth row needs a little algebra: opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add to 180°, so (2x + 10) + (3x − 20) = 180 gives 5x − 10 = 180 and x = 38. Then ∠P = 86° and ∠R = 94°, which do indeed add to 180°.

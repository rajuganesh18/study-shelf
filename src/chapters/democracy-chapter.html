Why it counts
—

Democracy is a form of government in which the source of power and authority lies with the citizens of the country, who elect their representatives through free and fair elections. It rests on freedom, equality, justice, rights and duties — and on citizens having the right to vote, to express their opinions and to take part in decision-making.

India, that is Bharat, is the world's largest democracy: a population of over 140 crore and a voter base of over 96.8 crore in 2024. Its democratic principles are guided by the Constitution of India, adopted on 26 November 1949 and in force from 26 January 1950, which guarantees and protects the Fundamental Rights of all citizens and ensures that no citizen is discriminated against on the basis of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.

Then decide: democratic, partially democratic, or non-democratic — and say why.
02Roots, and one mythConsultation, long before the word democracy30 XP

Pick everything the chapter actually says about India's early democratic traditions — and leave the three it contradicts.

From the earliest times the king was not an independent ruler: he worked in consultation with assemblies, ministers and officers of various capacities. The previous chapter met the Vedic assemblies — Sabha, Samiti and Vidhata — and the early republican states, the gaṇas or saṁghas.

The Bauddha Saṁgha, the monastic community established by Gautama Buddha, reflects the same tradition. It encouraged debate and discussion; its members could choose their leader and could take decisions by voting. That is collective decision-making with a procedure attached.

Aikyamatya Sūktam · Ṛigveda 10.191.3
Samāno mantraḥ samitiḥ samānī / samānaṁ manaḥ saha chittam eṣhām | — "Their counsel is one and the same; their assembly is one. Their mind is one, and their intention together is one." A hymn about consultation, consensus and shared responsibility, and one of the chapter's reasons for saying these values have deep roots in India's intellectual and cultural traditions.

Why it matters
—
—

Foreign rule had altered political structures and limited people's participation in their own governance. But the long struggle for independence revived and strengthened democratic ideas among the people — and the Assembly that met from 1946 drew on both: indigenous democratic traditions, and the global spread of democratic values.

The Constituent Assembly Debates show members who did not want a rigid legal code but a dynamic document, able to evolve through lawful amendments without compromising its fundamental values. So alongside the institutions that uphold liberty, equality, justice and fraternity, the Constitution provides for amendment under Article 368 — which is what keeps it responsive to changing socio-political needs.

The longest one there is
Two years, eleven months and eighteen days produced the world's longest written Constitution. Dr B.R. Ambedkar chaired the Drafting Committee.

Detail

Equality before law means every individual, irrespective of status, identity or rank, is treated equally by the law. Equal protection of law means all persons in similar circumstances shall be treated equally, without arbitrary discrimination. And procedure established by law — Article 21 — says "no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law".

The real strength of a democracy, the chapter insists, lies in its citizens: their participation, their awareness of duties and rights, and their commitment to honesty and fairness. Without these, no democratic process or institution can function effectively or deliver meaningful outcomes.

06Three organs, and what holds themSeparation of powers, and checks and balances35 XP

What it does
—
Held in check by

Separating the powers prevents the concentration of power in any one organ. Parliament can amend the Constitution — and the judiciary can review those amendments to check they are consistent with its spirit. The executive power of enforcement agencies is regulated by laws and rules framed by Parliament, and can also be reviewed by competent courts. If a law is inconsistent with constitutional provisions, the judiciary has the power to declare it unconstitutional.

The judiciary is an impartial and independent institution that safeguards citizens' rights and upholds the spirit of the Constitution, and it ensures that laws enacted by Parliament and the State Legislative Assemblies are in accordance with it. Initiatives such as Public Interest Litigation — a legal action started in a court for the enforcement of public interest — have been taken by the judiciary to ensure access to justice for all.

Think about it
What would happen if the same organ of government made the laws, implemented them and enforced them? Work through one law — a traffic rule, say — and ask at each step who could stop an abuse of it.

08Process, and the body that carries itThe chapter's own table, as a matching game30 XP

Seven democratic processes, seven sets of institutions that do the work. Pair them.

Tap a process, then tap the institutions that carry it.

Each of these carries its own democratic values. The legislative process gives representation, deliberation and dissent; the electoral process, participation and equality; the judicial process, rule of law, equality and justice; participatory processes, debate, discussion and freedom of expression; accountability mechanisms, transparency; decentralisation, people's participation at the grassroots; and federalism, the devolution of power.

The media has become an integral part of our lives. It keeps people informed about developments around the world and serves as a voice of the masses: newspapers, news channels and social media platforms raise issues concerning the public and contribute to their resolution through appropriate mechanisms. For that reason it is often called the fourth pillar of democracy.

Question Hour
The first hour of a parliamentary session, when members ask questions of the government. It is a small, regular, boring-sounding device — and it is one of the main ways a legislature holds an executive to account. The auditing of government accounts is another; the Right to Information is a third.

Part 4 · India's living democracy
11The scale of itFive numbers worth sitting with35 XP

More people vote in Indian elections than the entire population of many countries. Slide through the five figures the chapter prints.

The figure

What it shows
—

India's democracy stands out for its scale, depth and inclusiveness. The ability to conduct regular, nationwide elections for such a large and diverse population says something about the institutional strength and organisational capacity behind it.

What makes it living, though, is not the numbers but the engagement: an electorate drawn from varied linguistic, religious and socio-economic backgrounds, all participating inside a single democratic framework, so that elections become a collective national exercise. And democracy is not limited to elections — participatory democracy means citizens shaping policies and governance through local councils, public consultations and community-driven initiatives too.

12Democracy at the grassrootsTwo panchayats, and the law behind them30 XP

Pick everything the chapter reports about local self-government — and leave the three it does not.

India's constitutional framework operates through a three-tier system — Union, State and Local governments — which helps both administrative efficiency and democratic participation. Beyond the Panchayati Raj and Municipalities, the Constitution also protects tribal heritage: in specific north-eastern regions, Autonomous District Councils hold legislative and judicial authority to preserve tribal customs, and the PESA Act of 1996 — Panchayats Extension to Scheduled Areas — applies to tribal areas in other states, empowering the Gram Sabha as the primary decision-making body.

Cases like Jethipura and South Manubankul show that democracy in India is not limited to voting during elections. When citizens actively participate in grassroots and Panchayati Raj institutions, democracy becomes more responsive, inclusive and effective at every level. The spirit of democratic functioning extends beyond government too — civil society organisations, socio-religious and community institutions, cooperatives and trade unions often adopt democratic decision-making methods of their own.

Quorum
The minimum number of members who must be present at a meeting of an assembly for its proceedings to be valid. Jethipura was recognised partly for ensuring adequate quorum in its Gram Sabhas, with active participation from Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and women. A rule about attendance turns out to be a rule about who counts.
13Women and the right to voteThree countries, and two articles35 XP

In many countries women had to fight for decades for the vote. Tap through what happened where — and then what the Constitution reserves.

What happened
—
Detail
—
—

When the Constitution of India came into force in 1950, Universal Adult Franchise was extended to all citizens — men and women alike, without discrimination. Indian women did not have to engage in prolonged legal battles or protest to secure voting rights. In Britain women received full voting rights only in 1928, and in the United States in 1920, after decades of protests and movements.

But the right to vote does not automatically ensure equal participation. Social prejudices continue to act as barriers and affect women's adequate representation in politics — which is why empowerment starts with active participation in the institutions where decisions are made.

Part 5 · What can go wrong
14The Emergency, 1975–77Twenty-one months, one page at a time35 XP

One of the major challenges to democracy in India was recorded when an Emergency was imposed. Turn the page and watch what happens to it.

Where we are
—
Stage
0 of 6
—

In the early 1970s public dissatisfaction with the government led by Indira Gandhi was growing: rising unemployment, inflation and allegations of misgovernance led to widespread protests. In June 1975 a National Emergency was imposed on the grounds of internal disturbance. A majority of Fundamental Rights were suspended, the press was censored, and numerous political leaders and activists were arrested. Democratic institutions came under severe strain and citizens' freedom was restricted.

Mass movements led by Jayaprakash Narayan — a political leader and socialist thinker, popularly known as Lok Nayak — mobilised students and citizens, especially in Bihar and Gujarat. The Emergency was lifted in 1977, general elections were held, and the defeat of the ruling government demonstrated the strength of Indian democracy. The period showed both the vulnerabilities and the resilience of democratic institutions in India.

Three kinds of emergency
Articles 352, 356 and 360 of the Constitution deal with the declaration of a National Emergency, President's Rule and a Financial Emergency respectively. The provisions exist in the Constitution itself — which is exactly why the constitutional safeguards around them, and active citizen participation, matter so much.
15Strengthens, or weakens?Democracy is a habit, not an event30 XP

Democracy is not limited to elections — it shows up in everyday behaviour, decision-making and civic responsibility. Does each of these eight leave it stronger, or weaker?

India has made significant progress in ensuring representation and participation, but it continues to face real challenges: illiteracy, misinformation and inequality. The spread of fake news, especially through social media and other digital platforms, has emerged as a major concern — it can influence public opinion, create confusion and sometimes even lead to conflict. Poverty, regionalism, gender inequality and social discrimination create barriers to equal participation, and gaps in the effective implementation of laws and policies can reduce public trust in institutions.

Being well-informed is one of the vital responsibilities of a young citizen. Reading newspapers, watching news programmes and using the internet responsibly help you stay aware, form informed opinions and contribute positively. Civic responsibility also includes respecting others' rights, using social media responsibly, abiding by laws and rules, appreciating the country's diversity, and taking part in activities that strengthen unity — through the NSS, the NCC, the Bharat Scouts & Guides, or your own school and community.

Find two or three things shared on social media that you think may be misleading or false. What made you doubt them? How could they change someone's opinion? Then pick one activity this month that fulfils a civic responsibility, do it, and afterwards ask honestly what it contributed.

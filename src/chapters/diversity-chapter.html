Part 1 · The variety, and how to see it

Mountains in the north, desert in the west, rainforest in the north east, plateaus in the south, and two long coastlines. Each region has its own soil, temperature and rainfall — and its own species. Tap a region.

Why it counts

A biodiversity hotspot supports many endemic species and has already lost much of its habitat. Both halves of that definition matter.

A species restricted to one region of the world and found naturally nowhere else is endemic. The Nilgiri tahr, the lion-tailed macaque, the Indian pitcher plant Nepenthes khasiana and the Neelakurinji are all found only in India. Regions that support many endemic species and have undergone significant habitat loss are called biodiversity hotspots — the loss is part of the definition, which is why they are conservation priorities.

A deer that was declared extinct
The Sangai, Manipur's dancing deer, lives on phumdis — floating beds of rooted vegetation on Loktak lake, a few centimetres to two metres thick. It was declared extinct in 1951 and rediscovered in 1953. The phumdis are now degenerating and the Sangai is on the IUCN Red List.

Watch one animal — the tiger, say — as you switch criteria. It changes group every time.

The same organism fits into different groups depending on the criterion you choose. That is the observation that makes the whole chapter necessary: if any criterion will do, then grouping tells you nothing. So which criteria should scientists use?

The answer is that useful criteria are the ones that reflect shared ancestry. Similar features in organisms suggest they evolved from a common ancestor, and grouping on those features produces a classification that predicts other things about an organism rather than merely restating what you already noticed.

The seven the chapter names are external features, mode of nutrition, internal structures, cell structure, ecological role, reproduction and genetic similarity. Notice that "where it lives" is not among them — habitat was Aristotle's criterion, and it is exactly the one that had to be abandoned.

Classification systems keep changing, and that is not a weakness. Aristotle's system worked for his time; new tools — microscopes, staining techniques, and eventually DNA sequencing — revealed organisms he could not have known about. Biological classification is an ongoing process of reasoning and revision, not a finished list.

And it has changed again since
Comparing organisms at the DNA level, Carl Woese proposed the three domain system in 1977: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. It showed that microscopic life is far more diverse than anyone had believed — the split between two kinds of prokaryote turned out to be deeper than the split between a mushroom and a whale.

Each system added a kingdom to solve a specific problem: Protista for the unicellular eukaryotes, Monera for the prokaryotes once microscopes showed bacteria have no true nucleus, and Fungi once it was clear that mushrooms neither move like animals nor make their own food like plants.

The key runs on four questions in a fixed order: is the cell a prokaryote or a eukaryote; is the organism unicellular or multicellular; does it have a cell wall; and if it does, is the wall chitin or cellulose. Those four answers place any organism in one of the five kingdoms — and the order matters, because cell type is the most fundamental division and nutrition the least.

A slide shows a single-celled organism with a well-defined nucleus and many cilia. Which kingdom?
A well-defined nucleus rules out Monera, which is prokaryotic. Single-celled rules out Fungi, Plantae and Animalia, which are multicellular. A unicellular eukaryote is Protista — and cilia for movement fit Paramecium exactly.

Notice that no single feature separates all five. Cell type divides Monera from the rest; level of organisation divides Protista from Fungi, Plantae and Animalia; the cell wall divides Animalia off; and what the wall is made of, together with nutrition, separates Fungi from Plantae. Examples

Pick a kingdom. The single most useful question is different for each one.

Monera are unicellular prokaryotes — bacteria, archaea and cyanobacteria. They live everywhere, including inside us and in hot springs where almost nothing else survives. Some are pathogens; many are useful, like Lactobacillus and Rhizobium. Cyanobacteria are autotrophs, and some bacteria break down oil, pesticides and sewage.

Protista are single-celled eukaryotes — Amoeba, Paramecium, Euglena, Chlamydomonas — either without a cell wall or with one of cellulose. Some are autotrophic, some heterotrophic, and Euglena is both depending on the light. They are an important link in aquatic food chains.

Fungi are mostly multicellular eukaryotes with cell walls of chitin. They absorb nutrients from dead or decaying matter through fine filaments that form a network called a mycelium. Most are saprophytes and act as decomposers; some are symbiotic and some parasitic. Yeast is the exception that proves the rule — unicellular, but placed in Fungi because its wall is chitin.

The oxygen you are breathing
Cyanobacteria were among the first organisms to produce oxygen by photosynthesis. About 2.5 billion years ago that oxygen accumulated in the atmosphere and made the Earth suitable for other forms of life. Fossils of ancient cyanobacteria survive as layered structures called stromatolites, found in Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh — some of the earliest evidence of life on Earth.
09Sort them into kingdomsSection 12.6 · putting the key to work35 XP

Each of these is described by its features rather than named outright. Decide the kingdom.

The last one is the interesting case. A virus contains genetic material like a living organism but has no cellular organisation at all — no cell, no nucleus, no cell wall, and it cannot carry out any life process outside a host cell. Cell structure is the first question the key asks, and a virus cannot answer it. It therefore sits outside all five kingdoms, which tells you something real about the limits of classification systems.

From algae to angiosperms, plant groups show a sequence of structural changes that meet the challenges of life on land. Early plants relied on water for support and for reproduction. Gradually they evolved transport tissues, then seeds, then flowers and fruits. Each is a different way of balancing growth, transport, reproduction and survival.

Lichens are two organisms, not one
Those white-green patches on tree trunks and damp walls are mutual associations of an autotrophic alga and a heterotrophic fungus — the fungus provides protection and water, the alga photosynthesises and provides food. They change colour with air pollutants, which makes them natural bioindicators of air quality. Some are used as the spice patthar ke phool; some are poisonous, which is why identifying them properly matters.
11Which class, which featureTable 12.3 · salient features35 XP

Bryophytes are called the amphibians of the plant kingdom: they colonised land but their male reproductive cells must still swim to reach the female cells, so they need moisture to reproduce. Pteridophytes solved transport with xylem and phloem but not reproduction. Gymnosperms solved reproduction with seeds. Angiosperms added flowers and fruits, and became the most diverse plant group on Earth.

Part 5 · The animal kingdom

Sponges are multicellular but have no tissues at all. Cnidarians reach tissue level and can catch prey with tentacles instead of waiting for water to bring it — but they still have a single opening for both intake and waste. Flatworms add bilateral symmetry and a head end; roundworms add a second opening, so food moves one way through the body. Annelids add segmentation and a body cavity; arthropods add jointed appendages and a hard external skeleton; molluscs show what a soft body plus a shell can do; and echinoderms build an internal skeleton of calcium carbonate without ever having a notochord.

Only three of the eight have a skeleton of any kind, and two of those three carry it on the outside. An external skeleton protects, reduces water loss and gives muscles something to pull against — which is what let arthropods colonise dry, exposed land in a way annelids never could.

Pick a group. Each one is a different answer to the same problem: how to live where you live.

The notochord is a flexible rod-shaped structure. Animals that never have one are non-chordates or invertebrates. Animals that have one at least once in their life are chordates, and in many of them it is the precursor of the vertebral column. Protochordates such as Amphioxus keep a notochord but never develop a backbone — a primitive chordate that shows how animals with a notochord may have arisen from simpler forms.

Vertebrates have a vertebral column: an internal skeleton that supports the body and protects the brain and spinal cord. That internal framework allows a larger body, efficient movement, complex organ systems, advanced senses and coordinated behaviour. They are grouped into fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals on broad patterns of habitat, body covering and reproduction.

Adaptations are the outcome of structural change over long periods. Fins and gills let fish move and breathe in water; feathers and hollow bones make flight possible in birds; fat storage in camels and thick fur in polar bears support survival in extreme conditions; and in mammals the mammary glands are an additional structural and functional change that improves the survival of the young.

Part 6 · Finding one organism among millions

Roughly how many

Every lower group is part of the group above it. At each step down, fewer members — and more features shared.

A tiger is bagh in Hindi, puli in Tamil, tiger in English and tigre in French. To avoid that confusion, Carolus Linnaeus introduced binomial nomenclature in the 18th century: every organism gets a two-part scientific name in Latin or a Latinised form.

Three rules govern it. The name has two parts, genus then species. The genus begins with a capital letter and the species is written in lower case. And the whole name is written in italics when printed, or underlined when handwritten. So: Panthera tigris, Mangifera indica, Pisum sativum.

Which group has fewer members but more features in common — class or genus?
Genus sits far lower in the hierarchy, so it contains fewer organisms — and because every lower group is contained in the one above, its members share everything the class shares plus more besides. Narrower group, longer list of shared features. The two always move in opposite directions.
Named after where it was found
The Purple Frog, Nasikabatrachus sahyadrensis, from Kerala takes its species name from the Sahyadri Hills. It lives underground for most of the year and surfaces only during the monsoon to breed. Its discovery in 2003 helped scientists understand ancient amphibian groups and highlighted the need for conservation in the Western Ghats.

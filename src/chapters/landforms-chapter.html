What it is
—

The Earth is made up of three main layers: crust, mantle and core. The crust is the outermost layer on which we live. Below it lies the mantle, which is very thick and hot. The core is the innermost layer and is extremely hot and heavy.

The crust along with the upper part of the mantle forms the lithosphere, and it is this lithosphere that is broken into tectonic plates. Beneath the lithosphere lies the asthenosphere, which is semi-molten and allows the plates to move. Without that soft layer, nothing above it could slide.

A word to know
Landform — a natural feature on the Earth's surface formed by processes such as weathering, erosion, deposition and the movement of the Earth's crust. Mountains, valleys, plateaus, plains, deserts and coastal features are all landforms.
02What actually moves a plateFig. 2.2 · convection in the mantle25 XP

A tectonic plate is a slab of solid rock the size of a continent. Something has to push it. Put the chain of causes in order.

The movement of tectonic plates is caused by convection currents in the mantle. Heat from the Earth's core causes molten material in the mantle to rise, while cooler material sinks. This continuous movement creates currents that push and pull the tectonic plates, causing them to move in different directions.

Plate tectonics is a theory given by W. J. Morgan. According to it the outermost layer of the Earth is not one single piece but is broken into several large and small pieces called tectonic plates, which move very slowly — usually a few centimetres per year. There are three kinds: continental plates, which carry continents; oceanic plates, which carry ocean floors; and mixed plates, which carry both.

03Three kinds of plate boundaryConvergent, divergent, transform35 XP

Example

At a convergent boundary two plates move towards each other. When continental plates collide they form fold mountains, such as the Himalaya. When an oceanic plate collides with a continental plate the oceanic plate sinks beneath it, leading to volcanic activity and earthquakes.

At a divergent boundary plates move away from each other; magma rises from below and forms new crust, creating features such as mid-ocean ridges — the Mid-Atlantic Ridge is a good example. At a transform boundary plates slide past each other without creating or destroying crust, which mainly causes earthquakes, as along the San Andreas Fault in the United States.

Don't miss out
Not every volcano is fiery. The mud volcano at Baratang Island in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands is a rare natural wonder in India, where mud bubbles out under the pressure of natural underground gases.

Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur along plate boundaries, especially around the Pacific Ocean, in an area known as the Ring of Fire. The theory of plate tectonics is important precisely because it identifies earthquake- and volcano-prone regions in advance, which is where disaster management has to start.

Don't miss out
In early times earthquakes were known as bhūkampa, the shaking of the Earth. In the Bṛihatsaṁhitā, Varāhamihira dedicated a section to them, noting how changes in wind, rain, clouds, animal behaviour and planetary alignments could signal one. He attributed earthquakes to four elemental forces — Vāyu (wind), Agni (fire), Indra (heaven or thunder) and Varuṇa (water) — each linked to particular constellations and regions: an early attempt to blend observation with reasoning about a physical phenomenon.

Some of the major tectonic plates of the world are the Pacific, Eurasian, African, North American, South American, Indo-Australian and Antarctic plates. Plate tectonics plays a major role in shaping the Earth's surface: the movement of plates leads to the formation of mountains, valleys, ocean basins, volcanoes and earthquakes, and it explains the distribution of continents and oceans across the Earth.

The correlation is the whole point. Earthquakes and volcanoes do not fall randomly — they trace the edges of the plates, and they crowd most thickly around the Pacific.

Part 2 · Forces from outside
06Broken, or carried away?Fig. 2.8 · weathering and erosion35 XP

Weathering only breaks rock where it stands. Erosion is what moves the pieces. Sort every case — the first three labels are kinds of weathering, the fourth is not weathering at all.

Weathering is the process through which rocks on the Earth's surface break down into smaller pieces. It does not involve movement of the broken material, only the breaking down. There are three main types: physical weathering, where rocks break into smaller pieces because of temperature changes, frost or wind; chemical weathering, where minerals in rocks change through reactions with water, air or acids, producing new substances; and biological weathering, caused by plants, animals or micro-organisms — plant roots growing into cracks and splitting the rock apart.

Erosion is the process by which soil, rocks and other surface materials are worn away and carried from one place to another by natural agents such as water, wind, ice or waves. Unlike weathering, erosion involves movement. It shapes landforms and can both create and destroy features on the Earth's surface.

Verdict
—

Erosion affects many human occupations by changing the land and soil people depend on. For farmers it removes the fertile topsoil needed for crops, lowering yields. Near rivers and coasts it washes away land, houses and roads. In construction and mining it destabilises land and creates safety risks. Even tourism and fishing suffer when beaches, rivers and fertile land are destroyed. Erosion does not only shape the Earth's surface; it directly affects human labour and livelihoods.

Don't miss out
The Sindhu-Sarasvatī civilisation used sophisticated techniques for water management — contouring, bunding, terracing, dams and canals. Sanskrit texts document these practices: the Vedas, Kṛiṣhiparāśhara, Kauṭilya's Arthaśhāstra and specialised treatises such as the Vṛikṣhāyurveda. The Arthaśhāstra contains detailed guidelines on assessing and categorising land by fertility and productivity. The Zabo system in Nagaland is an integrated approach using earthen bunds on hillslopes, with check dams across small streams to slow the water, prevent soil erosion, allow sediment to settle and recharge groundwater.
Part 3 · The agents of gradation
08A river from source to seaUpper, middle and lower course35 XP

The same river does three completely different jobs along its length. Travel downstream and watch what it makes at each stage.

Course

What the river does
—
Landforms here
—

Rivers shape the land through erosion, transportation and deposition. In the upper course, steep gradients and strong erosive force cut V-shaped valleys, waterfalls and rapids. In the middle course the river begins to meander, forming oxbow lakes and floodplains as it loses energy and starts depositing sediment. In the lower course it slows further and deposits large amounts of sediment, forming deltas, levees and alluvial fans.

A waterfall forms in the upper course where hard rock resists erosion while softer rock below is worn away, creating a sudden drop. Waterfalls draw tourists, are sometimes used for hydroelectric power, and often hold cultural or religious significance.

Let's explore
A delta is fan-shaped land built where a river meets a sea, ocean or lake and drops the sediment it has carried. Deltas are highly fertile, ideal for rice and jute, and rich in fish because fresh and salt water mix. They carry dense settlements and busy trade — and they flood. Have you heard of the Sundarbans delta? Find out what makes it unique, and why tourists go there.

A meander is a winding curve in the middle or lower course of a river, formed by lateral erosion and deposition. As the river flows it erodes the outer banks of a bend, where the water runs fastest, and deposits sediment on the inner banks, where it runs slowest. The loop grows until the neck is so narrow that the river cuts straight through it, abandoning the bend as an oxbow lake.

Meanders matter to people because the fertile soil deposited along their banks supports agriculture, and villages and towns often develop on the gentle slopes beside them. Meandering rivers are also used for navigation, irrigation and, where the curves are scenic, tourism. The Grand Anicut, also known as Kallanai in Tamil Nadu, is an example of a river put to work for irrigation.

10Headland, cave, arch, stackFig. 2.15 · what the waves do next35 XP

—

Coastal erosion occurs when waves, tides and currents wear away the land along the coast. Common landforms are cliffs (steep rock faces formed as waves undercut the base of the coast), wave-cut platforms (flat areas left behind as cliffs retreat), caves (where waves erode weak parts of the rock), arches (when caves on opposite sides of a headland meet) and stacks (isolated pillars left standing after arches collapse).

Waves also build. A beach is made of sand, pebbles or rock deposited along the shoreline by wave action, tides and currents. Beaches draw tourists and support fishing, and they act as natural barriers against strong waves and coastal erosion, protecting the settlements behind them.

Glacial erosion carves U-shaped valleys (as glaciers widen and deepen river valleys), cirques (bowl-shaped hollows at the head of a glacier), aretes (sharp ridges between valleys), hanging valleys (where smaller glaciers meet larger ones) and fjords (deep narrow inlets where the sea floods a glacial valley).

Moraines are landforms built from the rock, soil and debris — called till — that a glacier carries and leaves behind when it melts. Lateral moraines form along the sides of a glacier, terminal moraines at its end, marking its furthest advance, and medial moraines where two glaciers meet and their lateral moraines join in the middle. Moraines often make fertile soil, and they dam natural lakes used for water supply, irrigation and sometimes hydroelectric power.

Think about it
A devastating flood struck the Chamoli district of Uttarakhand in February 2021. Many people and livestock lost their lives; buildings, roads, bridges and hydel projects were badly damaged and villages were cut off. Can you find out what caused a flood so sudden and so unexpected?
12What the wind buildsFigs. 2.21–2.23 · dunes and yardangs30 XP

Wind carries only the small grains, and it drops them wherever it slows. Which shape you get depends entirely on where the wind comes from.

—

Wind erosion happens when strong winds pick up and carry away loose sand and soil. It creates yardangs (streamlined rock ridges carved by wind), ventifacts (rocks polished and shaped by sandblasting), deflation hollows or blowouts (shallow depressions where loose material has been removed) and desert pavements (flat surfaces left after the finer particles have blown away).

Dunes are hills or ridges of sand formed by wind in deserts and along sandy coasts. They act as natural barriers against desertification and wind erosion, provide ground for tourism and adventure sports, protect coastal settlements from strong sea winds, and their sand is sometimes used for construction. Where a deflation hollow reaches down to the water table, an oasis appears.

At the surface

Underground water, especially in limestone and other soluble rocks, creates a distinctive landscape called Karst topography through chemical weathering and erosion. Its landforms are caves (hollows formed as acidic water dissolves rock), stalactites (icicle-shaped formations hanging from the ceiling), stalagmites (rising from the floor), sinkholes or dolines (depressions formed when the ground collapses into an underground cavity) and underground rivers flowing through cave systems.

These matter to people because caves and underground rivers supply fresh water, draw tourists, and in some places carry cultural or religious significance. Stalactites and stalagmites attract geologists and adventurers alike, making them valuable for both study and recreation.

Let's explore
Look at the landforms around your school or home and try to work out which agent made them — running water, glaciers, wind, waves or groundwater. Together these agents of gradation are continuously modifying landforms, lowering high areas and filling up low ones.
Part 4 · Living with the land
14Landforms wrote the historyWhere people settled, and why there30 XP

Almost every decision about where to build a city was really a decision about the shape of the ground. Match each landform to what it did to the people living on it.

Tap a landform, then tap what it made possible.

Landforms have played a major role in shaping the history of human civilisations. Rivers and fertile plains like those of the Ganga, Nile, Brahmaputra and Indus gave rise to agricultural societies and early cities. Mountains acted both as barriers and as protectors — the Himalaya shielded India from invasion but also allowed cultural exchange through passes like the Khyber. Deserts such as the Thar limited large settlements but encouraged trade routes, including the Silk Route. Coasts and harbours supported trade, travel and contact with distant lands, helping kingdoms in south India flourish.

Even today, wars, settlements, trade and cultural growth have all been deeply influenced by the land's physical features.

15Four disasters, and what starts themLandslides, avalanches, GLOFs, dust storms30 XP

Each of these belongs to a particular landform, and each has causes you can name in advance — which is what makes preparing for them possible. Sort every cause.

Landslides are caused by natural and human factors that make slopes unstable: heavy continuous rainfall, which seeps in, adds weight and reduces friction; earthquakes and volcanic eruptions that shake the ground; steep slopes and loose weathered rock; and human activity such as deforestation, mining, road construction and unplanned building on hillsides, along with poor drainage and improper land use.

Avalanches come from the sudden instability of snow on steep slopes — heavy snowfall in a short time resting on weak layers, a sudden rise in temperature that reduces friction, strong winds piling snow unevenly, and disturbances from earthquakes or from skiing, trekking and construction. GLOFs — Glacial Lake Outburst Floods — happen when rapid melting swells a glacial lake until its natural dam of ice or loose moraine collapses, often helped by heavy rain, an earthquake, an avalanche or a landslide striking the lake. Dust storms arise when strong winds lift dry loose soil, after prolonged drought, in desert and semi-arid regions where deforestation, overgrazing or poor farming have left the land bare.

The natural world is complex, and studying it in full detail is often impossible. To make sense of this complexity, science uses models — simplified ways of looking at real systems that focus only on what is most important for a given question.

Building such models involves making assumptions and deliberately ignoring certain details. The brand of the bat, the colour of the ball and the amount of grass on the field make no difference. The mass of the ball, and the speed and direction in which it has been hit, are very important. Air resistance, spin and the stitching at the seam have smaller effects that can be ignored in a simple model. Remember that these choices are not mistakes; they are done on purpose.

Meet a scientist
Science often begins by ignoring details. When physicist Meghnad Saha studied the light from stars, he did not try to model every atom, every reaction or every movement inside a star. Instead he treated the matter in the star as a hot gas, ignored many complex processes, and focused only on temperature, pressure and how atoms formed ions. That simplification allowed him to explain how the colour of stars is deeply connected to their temperature.
02Cycling home from schoolActivity 1.1 · let us model25 XP

You ride a bicycle home from school and want to work out how long it will take. Decide what to keep in and what to leave out.

Keep it, or ignore it?

In physics a moving car may be represented as a single point; in chemistry, atoms and molecules are drawn as spheres and bonds; in biology, cells are shown as diagrams highlighting key parts; in earth science the Earth may be treated as a smooth sphere layered into distinct regions.

When studying the motion of a falling object, air resistance may be neglected to understand the basic effect of gravity. When studying how the heart pumps blood, many individual cells are ignored so that the organ can be understood as a functioning system. As models get more complex, we add extra details for greater accuracy.

Part 2 · The language science uses
03A shared set of shorthandQuantities, symbols and units30 XP

Scientists across the world need to compare results without arguing about words. Match each quantity to the symbol that stands for it.

Tap a quantity, then tap its symbol and unit.

Many words we use in everyday life, such as force, work, cell or reaction, have specific meanings in science. These meanings are often very specific because scientific ideas must be communicated clearly and unambiguously.

To allow scientists across the world to describe observations, compare results and build ideas together, science uses a shared language of specific terms, symbols and units. Quantities such as mass, velocity, force and electric current are represented by symbols like m, v, F and I, each associated with a defined unit.

Threads of curiosity
Why is the speed of light denoted by 'c'? Scientific symbols often come from history and international agreement, not from convenience. The speed of light is denoted c because it comes from the Latin word celeritas, meaning speed. Today the speed of light is one of the physical constants, defined to be exactly 299792458 m/s.

In a well-known incident a passenger aircraft ran out of fuel mid-flight due to a mix-up in units. The ground crew miscalculated the fuel required, since they used the density of fuel in pounds per litre rather than kilograms per litre. The aircraft was about 15,000 litres short and luckily could glide to an emergency landing, which damaged the aircraft though there were no casualties. Using standard SI units everywhere avoids conversions and errors.

Threads of curiosity
When we buy rice or vegetables we expect a kilogram to mean the same amount everywhere. Imagine the confusion if we used different weights everywhere. Measurements are based on agreed international standards, not local objects or opinions. Standard units allow scientific results to be compared, and ensure fairness in daily life and trade.
05An equation is a statementMathematics as a language for thinking25 XP

Learning to use mathematics in science does not mean memorising equations. Put the steps of the approach in the order the chapter recommends.

Mathematics in science is not meant to be a hurdle. It is a language that helps us think more clearly about the world. An equation is not just a calculation tool; it is a compact statement about how certain things are related. Describing motion using distance, time and velocity lets us answer questions about where an object will be at a later moment.

In the same way, mathematical expressions describe rates of chemical reactions, patterns of population growth, or changes in energy within a system. If you focus first on understanding the situation and the quantities involved, equations begin to feel less like obstacles and more like helpful guides.

Part 3 · Laws, theories and predictions
06Law, theory or principle?Three words with specific meanings30 XP

These three words get used loosely in ordinary speech. In science each one means something particular. Sort each example.

A law usually describes a regular pattern observed in nature, often expressed using words or mathematical relationships — Newton's laws of motion explain the jerk felt when a bus stops suddenly. A theory goes a step further and provides an explanation of why those patterns occur, usually based on evidence gathered over time; the atomic theory explains how molecules are formed.

Principles are broad ideas that help us make sense in a given situation, such as the principle of conservation of energy being applied when climbing up the stairs.

07A theory is not a guessWhat makes science reliable25 XP

"It's just a theory." That sentence means something quite different inside science than it does outside it. Mark each statement.

In science, a theory does not mean a guess or an untested idea. It is an explanation based on careful testing and critical examination. These ideas are always open to improvement and often change as new evidence becomes available. This is a key feature of science that makes it reliable.

Even the most successful scientific theories have limits and may fail when new conditions are explored or when measurements become more precise. Such failures are not a weakness of science; in fact they are its greatest strength. When predictions do not match observations, scientists do not reject ideas based on opinion or belief, but only on evidence. No scientific theory is ever final and none is beyond question.

Threads of curiosity
A commonly circulated claim is that food should not be eaten during an eclipse because it becomes harmful. Disproof comes from asking simple scientific questions. An eclipse is just a play of shadows. What physical change occurs? Does temperature change significantly? Does food go bad if left in a shadow? You will conclude that no physical, chemical or biological mechanism supports such a claim.
08Make it testableExample 1.2 · checking a prediction30 XP

Varsha tells Meghna, "It will rain this afternoon because the clouds look dark." Pick the questions that would turn that into something checkable.

Good scientific questions look for measurable evidence and past patterns. Questions with simple yes or no answers are usually not so useful.

One of the most remarkable strengths of science is its ability to make predictions. When laws, theories and models are well established, they allow us to anticipate what will happen under new or different conditions, before we can perform an experiment. These predictions are not guesses; they are reasoned expectations based on evidence and careful thinking. When predictions match observations, confidence in the underlying science grows. When they do not, scientists re-examine their assumptions, models or measurements.

At rest we take about 12 to 15 breaths a minute, so roughly 18 to 22 thousand breaths a day. It takes about 4 or 5 breaths to fill a party balloon holding about 2 litres, so one breath is perhaps 0.5 litre. That gives about 10,000 litres of air a day.

A helpful strategy is to first understand the situation being studied, then identify the quantities that matter, and finally make a rough estimate to check whether an answer makes sense. Exact values are not always necessary. Learning to estimate helps you build intuition, detect errors, and develop confidence in your thinking. Science values careful reasoning perhaps much more than accurate calculations.

12How does a mask really work?The natural world has no branches30 XP

A surgical mask looks like one simple object. Working out how it stops a virus takes more than one subject. Pick every branch involved.

Understanding how a mask works requires concepts from physics (particle motion and electrostatic attraction), chemistry (properties of polymer fibres), biology (size and behaviour of viruses), and mathematics (modelling airflow and filtration efficiency).

After Grade 10, if you decide to study science, it will be divided into branches like physics, chemistry, biology and earth science. However, the natural world does not have any such boundaries. These divisions are made by us only to help organise knowledge; they are not independent of each other. Most real-world problems today, such as understanding climate change, developing medicines, or designing sustainable technologies, require ideas from several disciplines together.

Science is not just a collection of facts, equations or experiments. It is a human activity shaped by curiosity, creativity, collaboration and careful questioning. It grows as people ask questions, test ideas, share results, and learn from mistakes.

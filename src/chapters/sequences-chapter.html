A sequence is an ordered list of numbers, and each number in it is a term. Here are four you have met before. The quickest way to see what a sequence is doing is to look at the gaps between its terms.

Work through all four. Two of them have a constant gap, and two do not — that difference is the whole of the rest of the chapter.

The natural numbers step up by 1 every time and the odd numbers by 2: their differences are constant. The triangular and square numbers are not so obliging — their gaps are 2, 3, 4, 5 and 3, 5, 7, 9, which are themselves sequences. Notice what those second rows are: the triangular gaps are the natural numbers, and the square gaps are the odd numbers. Sequences are made out of other sequences.

The nth triangular number is a triangle of dots with rows of 1, 2, 3, … n, so it is 1 + 2 + ⋯ + n. The nth square number is an n by n square built from L-shaped shells of 1, 3, 5, … dots, so it is 1 + 3 + 5 + ⋯ + (2n − 1). Two very different-looking facts, and both of them are just a way of counting the same dots twice.

Pick a rule, then hunt for the target. A number belongs to the sequence only when solving for n gives a whole number.

With sn = 5n − 2, asking whether 308 is a term means solving 5n − 2 = 308, which gives n = 62 — a whole number, so 308 is the 62nd term. Asking the same of 471 gives n = 94.6, and there is no 94.6th position, so 471 is not a term at all. That is the test, and it is the reason n has to be a natural number.

05Virahāṅka counts the metresSection 8.3 · the sequence and where it came from55 XP

In the 7th century CE Virahāṅka was not doing arithmetic. He was counting Prakrit poetic metres: how many ways can a line of a given length be built from short syllables lasting one beat and long syllables lasting two? Here is his actual question, laid out.

Ways to fill it

Made from

Ratio to the last

Count the lines for 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 beats. The answers are 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 — and the reason is in the pictures.

Every line either starts with a short syllable, leaving n − 1 beats to fill, or starts with a long one, leaving n − 2. There is no third option and no overlap, so the count for n beats is the count for n − 1 plus the count for n − 2. That is the whole proof of Vn = Vn−1 + Vn−2, five centuries before Fibonacci wrote it down.

The ratio nobody was looking for
Divide each term by the one before it and the answers wander in towards 1.618…, the golden ratio — which is (1 + √5)/2, and is irrational. Virahāṅka was counting poems; Gopāla (c. 1135) and Hemachandra (c. 1150) took the sequence further; Fibonacci met it around 1200 while thinking about rabbits. None of them was chasing φ, and all of them found it.
Part 3 · Adding the same amount each time
06Which of these is an AP?Section 8.4 · the Think and Reflect40 XP

An arithmetic progression is a sequence where the difference between consecutive terms is the same every time. Six sequences from this chapter — decide which qualify.

The test is one thing only: subtract each term from the next and see whether you get the same number every time. A sequence can grow steadily and still fail it — 1, 4, 9, 16 climbs by 3, then 5, then 7, and the fact that those gaps form a pattern does not make the original an AP. Note also that a negative common difference is perfectly allowed: 11, 7, 3, −1 is an AP with d = −4.

07An AP is a straight lineSection 8.4.1 · Figs. 8.3 and 8.450 XP

Plot the position number against the term and something happens that is worth pausing over: the points fall exactly on a straight line. Every AP does this, and no other kind of sequence does.

Recursively

Set a = 1 and d = 4 for the growing squares of Fig. 8.3. Then make d negative, and then zero.

tn = a + (n − 1)d, which multiplied out is dn + (a − d) — an expression with n to the first power and nothing else, which is exactly what makes the graph a line. The common difference d is the slope: steeper for a bigger step, downhill when d is negative, and flat when d = 0, where the sequence just repeats one number forever.

The booking fee lifts the whole line without tilting it. The per-kilometre rate is what tilts it.

With ₹200 and ₹40/km the fares run 240, 280, 320, … — an AP with a = 240 and d = 40, so the nth fare is 240 + (n − 1) × 40, which tidies to 200 + 40n. Ten kilometres costs ₹600. The fee is the intercept and the rate is the slope, which is the same pair of jobs they had back in chapter 2's linear polynomials — the same idea meeting you again in different clothes.

Two rows of n numbers, each column adding to n + 1, make a rectangle of n(n + 1) — which is twice the sum. So Sn = n(n + 1)/2, which is also the formula for the nth triangular number, because they are the same thing counted the same way. Āryabhaṭa wrote this down in the Āryabhaṭīya, Chapter 2, Verse 19 — and gave a second recipe alongside it: take the first and last terms, average them, and multiply by how many there are.

Part 4 · Multiplying by the same amount each time
11Find the common ratioSection 8.6 · Examples 6 to 940 XP

In a geometric progression each term is the one before it multiplied by a fixed number, the common ratio. Divide any term by its predecessor and you have it.

Two of these are worth a second look. 1, −1, 1, −1, … is a perfectly good GP with r = −1 — a negative ratio makes the sequence alternate rather than grow, and it is still geometric. And a ratio between 0 and 1, like the 3⁄4 of 5, 15/4, 45/16, …, gives a GP that shrinks towards zero without ever arriving. Growing is not part of the definition; multiplying by a constant is.

12Adding against multiplyingSections 8.4.1 and 8.6.2 · Figs. 8.4 and 8.950 XP

Start two sequences at the same number. Let one grow by adding a fixed amount and the other by multiplying by a fixed amount. For a few terms they keep company. Then they do not.

GP at n = 10
—
They part at

Set the ratio below 1 and the GP shrinks instead. At exactly 1 it stops moving, and the AP walks away from it.

The AP's points lie on a line; the GP's do not, and cannot. Give the GP any ratio above 1 and it will overtake any AP eventually, however large that AP's common difference — not because it starts faster, but because multiplying compounds and adding does not. That single fact is why compound interest, bacterial growth and the number of triangles in a fractal all behave the way they do.

Part 5 · Patterns inside patterns
14Sierpiński's triangleSection 8.6.1 · Fig. 8.7 and Table 155 XP

Take a triangle. Join the midpoints of its sides and remove the middle one. Do the same to each of the three that are left, and keep going. Two sequences come out of this, and they run in opposite directions.

Black triangles
—
Area left
—
Both are GPs
—
Walk out to stage 6. The number of triangles multiplies by 3 each time; the area they cover multiplies by ¾.

Each black triangle becomes three at the next stage, so the count is 3n — a GP with r = 3, exploding. But each stage keeps only three of the four quarters, so the area is (¾)n — a GP with r = ¾, vanishing. More and more triangles, covering less and less. At stage 20 there are over three billion of them, occupying about 0.3% of the paper.

The same trick on a square
Exercise 8.3 question 7 does it with a square: cut it into nine, throw the middle one away, repeat. That gives 8n red squares covering (8⁄9)n of the area — the same two-GPs-in-opposite-directions shape, with different numbers. Wacław Sierpiński (1882–1969) described both; shapes that repeat themselves at every scale are called fractals, and they turn up in cauliflower, coastlines, bare trees and snowflakes.
15Work the progressionsEnd-of-chapter Q1, Q2, Q8, Q9, Q10, Q1345 XP

Six questions where you are told something about a progression and asked for something else. Each one comes down to writing what you know as equations in a and d, or a and r.

The pattern for the AP rows is always the same. “The 11th is 38 and the 16th is 73” means a + 10d = 38 and a + 15d = 73; subtracting gives 5d = 35, so d = 7 and a = −32. Five steps apart, thirty-five apart in value — the common difference falls straight out of the difference between two facts, which is why you subtract rather than substitute.

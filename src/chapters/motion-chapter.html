Move the fixed point without moving the athlete. Her position changes even though she has not.

That fixed point is the reference point. The distance and direction of the object from it, at an instant of time, describes the position of the object at that instant. Marked on a line, the reference point is called the origin 'O'.

Direction matters, so positions to the right of O are taken as positive and to the left as negative. If the position of an object with respect to the reference point changes with time, it is in motion. If it does not change with time, the object is at rest.

A tree stands 30 m to the left of a milestone. Taking the milestone as origin, what is the tree's position?
Position needs distance and direction. Left of the origin is taken as negative, so the tree is at −30 m. Drop the sign and you have thrown away half the answer.
Note
An instant of time and a time interval are not the same thing. An instant of time is a single reading of a clock at a given point of time. A time interval is the duration between two instants of time, that is, between two readings of a clock.

The total distance travelled is the whole length of path covered: OA + AB = 100 m + 60 m = 160 m. Displacement is the net change in the position of an object between two given instants of time — here just OB = 40 m.

Displacement needs both a direction and a numerical value with units. That numerical value is called its magnitude. For total distance travelled, only the numerical value is required, not the direction. The SI unit for both is the metre (m).

Note
For motion in a straight line, the total distance travelled and the magnitude of displacement are equal if the object moves without turning back, that is, if it moves in one direction.
Ready to go beyond
Physical quantities which can be specified by just their numerical value are called scalars. Physical quantities which require specifying both the direction and magnitude are called vectors. You will learn about these in higher grades.

04How fast, on averageAverage speed · uniform and non-uniform motion25 XP

Some things cover the same distance in every second. Others speed up and slow down as they go. Can you tell which is which?

Sort each one.

The average speed of an object is the total distance travelled divided by the time interval during which this distance is covered. Since distance travelled has no direction, average speed has no direction either — only a numerical value.

If an object moving in a straight line travels equal distances in equal intervals of time, for all possible choices of time intervals, it is in uniform motion in a straight line and moves at a constant speed. If it travels unequal distances in equal intervals of time, it is in non-uniform motion.

Two postmen start walking towards each other from 210 yojanas apart. One covers 9 yojanas a day, the other 5. After how many days do they meet?
Together they close 9 + 5 = 14 yojanas each day. 210 ÷ 14 = 15 days. In that time the first covers 135 yojanas and the second 75.
India's scientific contributions
The idea that speed is distance travelled divided by time taken is well established, dating back to ancient times in India, as seen in the treatise Aryabhatiya (5th century CE). The postmen problem above comes from the Ganitakaumudi, a comprehensive mathematical text of the 14th century CE.
05Speed or velocity?Average velocity · rate of change30 XP

Sarang swims from one end of a 25 m pool to the other and back again, taking 50 seconds in all.

For each journey, tap each box to cycle it: blank → ✓ (yes, it is zero) → ✗ (no, it is not zero).

The average velocity of an object in a time interval is the change in position, or displacement, divided by the time interval in which that change occurs. Written out: vav = s / t.

Sarang travels 50 m but his displacement is 0 m, so his average speed is 1 m s−1 while his average velocity is 0 m s−1. The SI unit of both is metre per second, written m s−1 or m/s. It is also commonly measured in kilometre per hour, km h−1.

The ratio of change in one quantity to the corresponding change in time is called the rate of change. So average velocity is the average rate of change of position with respect to time.

Note
For motion in a straight line, the average speed and the magnitude of average velocity in a time interval are equal if the object moves in one direction.

The average acceleration of an object over a time interval is the change in its velocity divided by the time interval. If velocity changes from u at time t₁ to v at time t₂, then a = (v − u) / (t₂ − t₁).

The SI unit of average acceleration is m s−2 or m/s². Like displacement and velocity, we need to specify the magnitude as well as the direction. If the magnitude of velocity is increasing, the average acceleration is in the direction of velocity. If the magnitude of velocity is decreasing, the average acceleration is opposite to the direction of velocity.

Threads of curiosity
The reading of a vehicle's speedometer is nearly, but not exactly, the same as the magnitude of the velocity at an instant, while the direction of the tyres gives the direction of velocity at that instant.
07Fast is not the same as acceleratingExample 4.4 · falling under gravity25 XP

A bus on a straight highway holds a steady 80 kilometres an hour. Is it accelerating?

Decide for each one.
Acceleration depends not on how fast an object is moving, but on how quickly its velocity is changing. A bus on a straight highway at constant velocity has zero acceleration, even though its velocity may be high.

Drop an object from a height and it takes a straight vertical path downwards. Its velocity at each second is 0, then 9.8, then 19.6, then 29.4, then 39.2 m s−1. Every one-second interval adds the same 9.8 m s−1, so the average acceleration is constant at 9.8 m s−2. This is called the acceleration due to gravitational force by the Earth and is denoted by g.

In that example the origin was put at the point the object was dropped from, and downwards was taken as positive. Was that allowed?
We can choose the origin and the positive direction as per our convenience. However, once chosen, it should not be changed while solving a problem. In Fig. 4.5 the origin sat at ground level; in Fig. 4.10 it sat at the release point.
Ready to go beyond
Similar to 'velocity at an instant', 'acceleration at an instant' is known as instantaneous acceleration. In this chapter we consider only the cases where the acceleration is constant.

A straight line position-time graph indicates that the object is moving with a constant velocity — equal displacements in equal intervals of time. A curved position-time graph indicates that the velocity is not constant, and thus the object is in accelerated motion.

A position-time graph is a straight line parallel to the time axis. What is the vehicle doing?
The position never changes with time, so the vehicle is at rest at that position. A straight line parallel to the time axis on a position-time graph represents a stationary object.

The slope of a graph gives information about the rate of change of the quantity shown on the Y-axis with respect to the quantity shown on the X-axis.

Ready to go beyond
For the case of a curve also, the velocity at any instant can be calculated geometrically from the position-time graph. You will learn how to do it in higher grades.

The slope of the line on a velocity-time graph gives how fast the velocity is changing with time, that is, the acceleration. Where the line is flat the slope is zero, so the velocity is constant and the acceleration is zero.

The area enclosed by the velocity-time graph and the time axis for a desired time interval is equal to the displacement in that time interval. For a sloping line, split the shape: area of the rectangle plus area of the triangle.

Note
All the graphs in this chapter are for motion in a straight line in one direction only. In this special case distance travelled and magnitude of displacement are equal, and speed and magnitude of velocity are also equal.

These are the kinematic equations. They relate displacement (s), time interval (t), initial velocity (u), final velocity (v) and acceleration (a), and provide a mathematical description of how the motion of an object changes with time.

A car braking at −4 m s⁻² comes to rest from 15 m s⁻¹. You want the distance travelled but you are not told the time. Which equation do you reach for?
Only the third equation leaves out t altogether, so it is the one that works when time is unknown. Here 0 = 15² − 8s, giving s = 225/8 = 28.1 m.
Note
These kinematic equations are valid only when the acceleration is constant. In motion in a straight line in both directions, the sign of u, v, a and s tells us about the direction of that particular quantity.

14Keeping a safe distanceWhat changes the stopping distance25 XP

Same bus, same road, same driver. Change one thing at a time and say which way the stopping distance moves.

Longer, or shorter?

When brakes are applied to a moving vehicle, it moves some distance before coming to a stop. The distance travelled depends upon the velocity of the vehicle when the brakes are applied, the road surface, the braking capacity of the vehicle, and the driver's reaction time. That is why a safe distance from the vehicle ahead has to be adjusted to your speed.

As the number of sides increases, the athlete has to take turns more and more frequently. Finally the track approaches a circle, with each side decreasing to a point and the direction of the athlete's velocity changing continuously.

When an object moves in a circular path with constant, uniform speed, its motion is called uniform circular motion. If the radius is R and one revolution takes time T, the average speed is 2πR/T, while the average velocity over that revolution is zero, since the displacement is zero.

Acceleration is non-zero if the velocity of an object changes — and velocity changes if either its magnitude or its direction changes. In uniform circular motion the speed is constant at every point on the circle; it is only the direction of velocity that changes. So the motion is accelerated.

Ready to go beyond
The velocity at a point is along the tangent to the circle at that point, in the direction of motion. A straight line that meets the circle at one and only point is called a tangent to the circle at that point. In the real world the conditions for uniform circular motion — constant speed and a circular path — are often not met, so it is an idealised model.

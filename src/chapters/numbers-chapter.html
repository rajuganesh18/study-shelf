This is one-to-one correspondence, and it is older than counting words. The pot does not know what "seven" means; it only knows that every cow had a pebble and every pebble had a cow. Matching one collection against another is the act that Natural Numbers — ℕ = {1, 2, 3, 4, …} — were invented to record.

Three columns, three different ideas. One of them is a list of primes.

The Lebombo bone from the Lebombo Mountains is about 35,000 years old and carries 29 deliberate, uniformly cut notches — very likely a lunar phase counter. The Ishango bone, from the headwaters of the Nile and about 20,000 BCE, is stranger: one of its columns groups the notches into 11, 13, 17, 19 — every prime between 10 and 20, and nothing else.

The next three in that sequence
Exercise Set 3.1 asks what 11, 13, 17 and 19 have in common and what comes next. They are the primes between 10 and 20; the next three primes are 23, 29 and 31. Whether the carver knew what a prime number was, nobody can say — but the grouping is not what random scratching looks like.
Part 2 · Śūnya: when nothing became something
03Brahmagupta's rules for zeroSection 3.2.2 · 628 CE40 XP

A symbol is just a mark on a page until it has rules. Brahmagupta gave zero its rules in the Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta, and we still use them unchanged. Pick a number and an operation.

0 of 3
Three rules. Two leave the number exactly where it was; one collapses it to nothing.

Brahmagupta defined zero as what you get by subtracting a number from itself — a − a = 0 — and then laid down the arithmetic: a + 0 = a, a − 0 = a, and a × 0 = 0. Adding nothing changes nothing; taking nothing away changes nothing; but taking a number no times at all leaves you with nothing.

04From emptiness to a numberSection 3.2 · the road to śūnya35 XP

Zero did not arrive as arithmetic. It arrived as philosophy, and took centuries to become a number. Put the stages in order.

In the Upanishads and in Buddhist literature well before the 7th century BCE, śūnyatā — emptiness — described the state a meditator is trying to reach: a mind emptied of all vṛttis. Because Indian thinkers already revered that state, they had the conceptual framework to welcome nothingness as a thing. The Bakhshālī manuscript shows the physical step: a bold dot, a bindu, standing where nothing is. Brahmagupta gave it rules.

06The five laws, in his wordsSection 3.3.140 XP

Brahmagupta wrote the rules for signed arithmetic in the language of wealth and debt. Match each law to the sum that shows it.

Every one of these is still exactly how we compute today, 1,300 years later. The wording is the part that has changed: where he wrote "the product of two debts is a fortune", a modern textbook writes "negative × negative = positive" — and loses the reason along with the words.

07Why two debts make a fortuneSection 3.3.1 · Think and Reflect45 XP

"Negative times negative is positive" is a rule most people memorise and nobody enjoys. Here is the reason, in Brahmagupta's own terms: if a negative number is a debt, then multiplying by a negative number is the removal of that debt. Take some debts away and watch what happens to you.

Removing four debts of ₹3 is written (−3) × (−4), and it leaves you ₹12 richer: (−3) × (−4) = +12. Giving you four debts of ₹3 is (−3) × 4 = −12, and it leaves you ₹12 poorer. The sign rule is not a convention someone chose — it is what has to be true if the arithmetic is to match what happens to your money.

The book's two cases are here. 3/4 divides 0 to 1 into four parts and steps three of them. 9/4 is 2¼, so it sits one quarter past 2 — a fraction greater than 1 is placed exactly the same way, you simply run past the first unit. And a negative numerator steps the same distance the other way.

Absolute value: distance, not direction
|x| is how far x is from 0, so |5/3| = 5/3 and |−5/3| = 5/3 as well, while |0| = 0. It is never negative. And the distance between two numbers a and b is |a − b| — the gap between −4 and 3 in Fig. 3.8 is |−4 − 3| = 7, whichever way round you subtract.

The book's own chain starts with 1 and 2, whose average is 3/2; then 1 and 3/2 average to 5/4; and so on without end. This is what it means to say the rationals are dense: between any two of them there are infinitely many more. It feels as though they must fill the line completely, leaving no gaps whatsoever. They do not — and the next part is about what is missing.

12Rational arithmeticExercise Set 3.3 · Q2 to Q540 XP

Sum, difference, product and quotient. Give each answer in its lowest terms.

To add or subtract, first make the denominators the same; to multiply, go straight across the top and the bottom; to divide by c/d, multiply by d/c instead. All four keep you inside ℚ — the rationals are closed under every one of them, with the single exception of dividing by zero.

Part 5 · The gaps rationals cannot fill
13√2 cannot be a fractionSection 3.5.1 · proof by contradiction45 XP

A square of side 1 has a diagonal of length √2, by Baudhāyana's own theorem: 1² + 1² = d². Baudhāyana met such lengths around 800 BCE while building fire altars, and they defied every fraction he tried. Here is Hippasus's proof that they always will. Put the steps in order.

The shape of the argument is proof by contradiction: assume the opposite of what you want, follow it faithfully, and arrive at a logical disaster. We deduced that p is even and q is even, so they share a factor of 2 — but we began by saying p/q was in its simplest form, with no common factor at all. Both cannot be true. Since every step after the assumption was flawless, the assumption is what has to go.

The square root spiral
The same construction repeated builds every √n. Start with a right triangle of legs 1 and 1, whose hypotenuse is √2; erect a new leg of length 1 on that hypotenuse and the next one is √3; then √4, √5, √6 … Each triangle shares its long side with the one before, and the whole thing coils outward — Fig. 3.14's square root spiral, which is a picture of the irrationals arriving one at a time.
14Turning a repeating decimal back into p/qSection 3.6.1 · Examples 5 to 950 XP

Every repeating decimal is a rational number, and there is an algebraic trick that finds the fraction. Shift the decimal until two copies line up, subtract, and the infinite tail cancels itself out.

For a pure repeating decimal like 0.6, multiply by 10ⁿ where n is the length of the repeating block, then subtract: 10x − x = 6.6 − 0.6 = 6, so 9x = 6 and x = 2/3. For a general one like 0.16, first shift the non-repeating digits past the point (×10), then shift one whole cycle more (×10 again), and subtract: 90x = 15, so x = 1/6.

0.999… is exactly 1
Run the same machinery on 0.9. Let x = 0.9, so 10x = 9.9, and subtracting gives 9x = 9, hence x = 1. Not "nearly 1" — equal to 1. It is the same fact as 1 = 10/10 = 100/100: a number can have more than one decimal name, and every terminating decimal has a twin ending in repeating 9s.
15Terminate, or repeat forever?Section 3.6.1 · the prime-factor test50 XP

Find a terminating one, a repeating one, and the cyclic block of 1/7.

The decimal of p/q in lowest terms terminates precisely when the only prime factors of q are 2s and 5s — because then you can multiply top and bottom into a power of 10. 3/20 has 20 = 2² × 5, so 3/20 = 15/100 = 0.15. Any other prime in the denominator and the long division can never reach a remainder of 0, so the digits must eventually loop.

Why it has to loop
Dividing by 7, the only possible remainders are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 — six of them, and never 0. Keep going and a remainder must eventually show up a second time; the moment it does, the whole division repeats itself. That is why 1/7 = 0.142857, with a block exactly six long. And that block is cyclic: multiply 142857 by 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6 and the very same digits come back around in the same circular order.

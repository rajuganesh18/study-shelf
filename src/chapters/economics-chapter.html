This is the Production Possibility Curve: the curve showing the different combinations of goods that can be produced using all available resources. As the farmer moves from point A to point E, they produce more barley and less wheat. To grow more barley, some wheat must be sacrificed — and that sacrifice is the opportunity cost of growing barley.

Every point on the curve shows the maximum output that can be produced through the efficient use of resources, by avoiding wastage. That is what makes it useful: it turns a vague sense of "we can't have everything" into a specific list of what is actually available, which is what enterprises and governments need in order to plan.

The cost is not constant
Run the slider again and watch the last readout. The first 25 kg of barley costs 10 kg of wheat. The last 25 kg costs 40. That is arithmetic straight off the chapter's own table — and it is why the line bends instead of running straight.
02Unlimited wants, limited everything elseNeeds, wants, resources and the cost of choosing30 XP

Pick everything the chapter says — and leave the three it contradicts.

Some of our preferences are needs — essentials like food, water and shelter. Others are wants: gadgets, vacations, luxury items. Human wants are unlimited and keep changing; people may want to upgrade from a bicycle to a motorbike, and then to a car.

Resources are the factors used for the production of goods and services. They can be natural, like water and coal, or human-made, like capital and technology — and either way they are limited in quantity. They can also be put to many alternative uses, so choosing one use means giving up the others. The value of what is given up is the opportunity cost.

Let's explore
List three things your parents bought this month. Can you sort them into needs and wants? And a harder one: do you think having too many wants may create problems — for a household, and for the world the resources come out of?

Part 2 · What the subject actually is
04Eight words, preciselyThe chapter's own definitions30 XP

Economics is a subject where the everyday meaning of a word and the technical one drift apart. Pair each term with the definition the chapter gives it.

Tap a term, then tap its definition.

The word Economics itself comes from the Greek oikonomia, made of oikos — usually translated as household — and nemein, best translated as management. Economics is, at root, household management. With limited resources and unlimited wants, not just families but nations must plan how to use what they have efficiently.

The discipline deals with how choices are made by optimising the use of limited resources to satisfy needs and wants, and with how different economic entities — consumers, producers, governments and financial institutions — interact in an economy: how people work and earn wages, how wealth is distributed, how prices are determined, how education and technology drive investment, how policy and trade move prices and employment.

Good decisions rely on data and analysis, not guesswork. Families allocate money between essential items — food, medicines, school supplies — non-essential items like jewellery, entertainment and restaurant meals, and savings. Governments use revenue from taxation to plan expenditure on infrastructure and welfare programmes. Enterprises study market trends and new innovations to serve customers better and maximise profits.

Economists study the available alternatives, the opportunity costs attached to each, and the potential outcomes. They work from real records: government reports such as economic surveys, and the financial statements of companies. The analysis is what turns a list of options into an understanding of the risks and opportunities in each.

The Economic Survey of India
An annual document prepared by the Ministry of Finance and presented in Parliament before the Union Budget. It reviews the past year's economic performance, analyses agriculture, industry and services along with employment, inflation, education, health and infrastructure, and discusses the challenges and opportunities ahead. It is a blueprint for the Budget that follows — and it is written to be readable by citizens, not only by policymakers.

Any mismatch between unlimited wants and limited resources gives rise to scarcity, and scarcity forces choices. Those choices are what the three key questions of economics are about: what to produce, how to produce, and for whom to produce.

The rest of this chapter is the three questions, one at a time — and then the three different ways a country can organise itself to answer them.

Part 3 · The three questions
07What to produce: sugarcane, or millets?Short-term gain against long-term soil30 XP

The chapter's own example of the first question. Name the crop each line belongs to.

All seven rows must be answered before it will mark.

Should farmers produce water-intensive crops such as sugarcane and paddy, or drought-resistant crops such as millets and pulses? Sugarcane yields high profits and supports industries such as sugar. Millets and pulses save water, improve soil health and promote sustainable agriculture.

So the opportunity cost of producing sugarcane is the forgone gains from saved water and improved soil health. That is the whole shape of the question: a trade-off between short-term economic gains and long-term sustainability, made by accounting for the opportunity cost of each alternative. Companies, governments and consumers face the same kind of trade-off.

Think about it
Should the government allocate more funds to healthcare and education, or to defence and space exploration? Whichever you answer, say what the opportunity cost of your answer is — that is the part that makes it an economic argument rather than a preference.

The question of for whom to produce asks about the purpose of the goods produced, how they are distributed, and who benefits. Since resources are limited and people have different needs, income levels, tastes and lifestyles, producers decide which group of consumers they want to serve.

That decision reaches all the way down into the materials. Leather shoes are generally aimed at office-goers and high-income customers; rubber or synthetic shoes at sports players, factory workers, and people who need footwear that is affordable and durable. Producers analyse what consumers like, how much money they have, and how much demand there is, before deciding what to make — which is how limited resources end up being used well rather than wasted.

Producers must select the right mix of the factors of production — land, labour, capital and technology. Production can be labour-intensive, using more workers and less machinery, or capital-intensive, using more machines and technology and fewer workers. Agriculture and handicrafts usually rely more on labour; industries such as steel and automobile manufacturing depend more on machinery.

Which way a particular firm goes depends on the cost of capital, the technology available, and the nature of the product — along with the availability and relative cost of each factor, and the country's laws and regulations.

The garment factory
If machines are expensive, the firm relies more on labour; if machines become affordable, it may shift to automation. Advanced technology encourages machine use; limited technology leads to manual production. Customised or designer clothes need skilled labour; mass-produced garments suit machines. Cheap and plentiful labour favours labour-intensive methods; costly or scarce labour makes machines more efficient. And labour laws or incentives for machinery can tip the whole calculation either way.

In an economy, the answers to the three key questions depend on how the resources are organised and who controls the decision-making about them. That is what an economic system is.

In practice the three are not tidy boxes. Most economies have features of mixed systems — private ownership, with some degree of government regulation. Even market economies like the United States and Singapore have significant government involvement, and the chapter is explicit that governments play an important role even in the economies it lists as market ones.

Let's explore
Should the government stay out of enterprise decisions completely? And can you think of a case where government action clearly helped an industry — or clearly harmed one? The second question is the harder one, and the more useful.

Fig. 8.2 makes one point and makes it well: a resource has alternative uses. Steel can go to medical equipment, to refrigerator manufacturing, or to aircraft manufacturing. It is the same steel, and there is a limited quantity of it.

That is what connects a resource to a cost. Because the uses compete, sending the steel one way is also a decision not to send it the other two. The opportunity cost is not an extra charge somebody adds on — it is simply the value of the alternative you did not take, and it exists whether or not anyone bothers to calculate it.

In the decades after Independence, India followed a more state-led approach, similar to a planned economy. The government controlled industries, allocated resources and regulated production through licences and permits, and key sectors such as banking, transport and heavy industries were dominated by the public sector.

By 1991 the country faced serious economic difficulties. The government introduced major reforms that reduced excessive regulations, encouraged private enterprise, opened the economy to global trade and investment, and increased competition. These gradually shifted India towards a more market-oriented system — while still retaining an important role for the government.

A mixed economy combines features of both systems. Private individuals, enterprises and the government all play important roles in making economic choices, and large public sector companies play a very important role in the market alongside private ones.

Public goods are the clearest case of something the market side will not supply on its own: goods and services available to all, where one person's use does not prevent another's — parks, roads, police services, street lights, basic education. Nobody can be excluded from them, which is exactly why nobody can profitably sell them.

Why pure systems are rare
The chapter's own closing exercise asks you to examine this critically: why do pure economic systems rarely exist in reality, what are the limitations of each, and why is a mixed economy so often considered the more practical approach? Every answer worth giving will use the word trade-off.

Part 1 · What is force?
01Push, pull, squeezeActivity 6.1 · what a force can do30 XP

A force can start motion, stop it, change its direction, or change the shape of an object. It is a push or a pull, and it always has a magnitude and a direction.

Effect
—
Try all three to see the different effects of a force.

The SI unit of force is the newton (written with a small 'n'), symbol N. One newton is the force that produces an acceleration of 1 m s⁻² on an object of mass 1 kg.

Force is a physical quantity for which we need to specify both magnitude and direction. If either changes, the effect of the force changes.

A spring balance reads 5 N when you pull it. What exactly does the '5 N' tell you?
The reading gives the magnitude of the force in newtons. The spring balance measures force, not length or mass directly — though weight (a force) and mass are related by F = mg.
02The net force builderExample 6.1 · adding forces on a block30 XP

More than one force usually acts on an object at the same time. The combined effect is the net force — the single force that would have the same effect as all the individual forces together.

Net force
—
State
—
Drag both sliders. When two forces act in the same direction they add; when opposite they subtract.

When two forces are opposite in direction and equal in magnitude, the net force is zero. Such forces are called balanced forces — the object's state of motion does not change.

When the forces are unbalanced, there is a non-zero net force and the object accelerates in the direction of the net force.

03Balanced or unbalanced?Tug of war, weightlifter and more25 XP

In each situation, decide whether the forces are balanced or unbalanced.

A weightlifter holding a barbell steady is applying a force equal to the weight of the barbell upwards. The gravitational force acts downwards. The two are balanced — net force is zero and the barbell does not accelerate.

A ball rolling to a stop on a rough surface is slowing down — friction is an unbalanced force opposing the motion.

04Free-body diagramIdentify forces on a box on a table30 XP

A box is being pushed across a table. Apart from the applied force, at least three other forces act on it. Can you identify all four?

Tap a description, then tap the force it names.

Every object near the Earth's surface has at least two forces on it: gravity (its weight, pulling it down) and the normal force from the surface it rests on, pushing it up. When you push the box, friction opposes the push. The box moves only if the push is larger than friction — an unbalanced net force.

Part 2 · Friction: always present
05Coins and a rubber bandActivity 6.1 · friction on four surfaces35 XP

The force of friction acts between the surfaces in contact, in the direction opposite to the direction of motion. When friction is smaller, the coins travel further and decelerate more slowly.

A spring balance can measure friction directly: pull a block at constant velocity and the spring balance reading equals the force of friction (Activity 6.2).

06Friction: friend or foe?When friction helps and when it hinders25 XP

Friction is not always something to overcome. Without it, you could not walk, write, or hold anything. Sort these into helpful or hindering.

Grooves on shoe soles, treads on tyres, chalk dust on a gymnast's hands — these all increase friction deliberately. Oil in an engine, ball bearings in a wheel, a polished slide — these reduce it. Both matter.

What if…
If friction vanished entirely, you could not walk — your foot would slip on the ground as if on perfect ice. No brakes would work, no screw would hold, and a ball kicked once would never stop.
Part 3 · Newton's first law
07Galileo's thought experimentWhat happens as friction approaches zero?35 XP

A rubber band launches the coins from point C on the table. On a rough surface they stop quickly. On a smoother surface they travel further. Galileo asked: what if the surface were perfectly smooth?

Distance
—
Final speed
—
Drag friction towards zero and watch what happens.

As friction decreases, the coins travel further and slow down less. At zero friction, they would never stop — they would keep moving at constant velocity forever.

This is Newton's first law of motion: an object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues to move with a constant velocity, unless a net force acts upon the object.

The tendency of an object to resist any change in its state of rest or of uniform motion is called inertia.

A toy car of mass 100 g moves at constant velocity of 0.5 m s⁻¹ on a smooth surface. What is the net force acting on it?
Constant velocity means no acceleration, and by Newton's first law, if acceleration is zero the net force must be zero. Force is not needed to keep something moving — only to change how it moves.
08First law in actionInertia in everyday life25 XP

Newton's first law explains many everyday experiences. Match each situation to the correct explanation.

Inertia depends on mass: a heavier object resists change in motion more than a lighter one. That is why it is harder to push a loaded cart than an empty one, and why a fast-moving truck causes more damage than a bicycle at the same speed.

09Graphs of zero net forceExample 6.3 · what do the graphs look like?30 XP

When no net force acts on an object, there are only two possibilities: it is at rest, or it moves with constant velocity. Both produce distinctive graphs.

Tap each scenario to see its position-time and velocity-time graphs.
An object's position-time graph is a straight line sloping upward. What can you say about the net force?
A straight line on a position-time graph means constant velocity. By Newton's first law, constant velocity means zero net force. A curved position-time graph would indicate acceleration — and that requires a net force.
Part 4 · Newton's second law
10The F = ma benchActivities 6.3 and 6.4 · force, mass and acceleration40 XP

A cart on wheels is pulled by a falling cup attached via a thread and pulley. The cup's weight provides a constant force. Change the force or the mass of the cart and watch the acceleration change.

Acceleration

F = m × a
—
Try doubling the force while keeping mass the same. Then double the mass while keeping force the same.

Newton's second law of motion: when a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force. The magnitude of the acceleration is proportional to the force and inversely proportional to the mass.

F = m a

The acceleration due to gravity, g, is 9.8 m s⁻² near the Earth's surface. So the gravitational force on an object of mass m is F = mg. This is the object's weight.

Threads of curiosity
The force of 1 N feels roughly like holding a 100 g apple in your palm. Forces we can directly feel start around a millinewton (10⁻³ N). Scientists measure forces as small as yoctonewtons (10⁻²⁴ N) in specialised experiments.
12Catching and crashingWhy fielders pull their hands back25 XP

Newton's second law explains why the same change in motion can require very different forces, depending on how much time you allow.

Deceleration
—
Force on ball
—
A 0.15 kg cricket ball at 30 m/s. Drag the stopping time and see how the force changes.

A fielder pulling their hands back increases the time over which the ball's velocity drops to zero. Longer time means smaller deceleration, which means smaller force on both the ball and the fielder's hands.

The same principle explains airbags (cushion the stop over a longer time), crumple zones in cars, and why a coconut cracks when it hits a hard floor (very short stopping time, very large force).

Glass items are packed with bubble wrap for transport. How does this protect them?
Bubble wrap extends the stopping time during a collision. By F = ma, the same change in velocity over a longer time means smaller acceleration, and therefore a smaller force on the glass.
Part 5 · Newton's third law
13The balloon rocketActivity 6.7 · action and reaction35 XP

Inflate a balloon, tape a straw to it, thread the straw onto a string stretched between two points, and release the neck. The air rushes out one way; the balloon moves the other.

Air goes
—
Balloon goes
—
Inflate the balloon, then release.

The balloon pushes the air out (action). The air pushes the balloon in the opposite direction (reaction). The two forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction — but they act on different objects.

A real rocket works exactly the same way: burning fuel produces hot gas expelled downward; the gas pushes the rocket upward with an equal and opposite force.

Chandrayaan-3
The Vikram lander of Chandrayaan-3 fired its engines in the direction of motion to slow down before landing near the south pole of the Moon — the exhaust pushed forward, the reaction pushed the lander backward, reducing its speed for a soft touchdown.
14Action-reaction pairsMatch the force to its partner30 XP

Newton's third law: whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on the first. Match each action to its reaction.

The key point: the two forces in a Newton's third law pair act on different objects. They never cancel each other out, because they are not on the same object. If you push a wall, the wall pushes you — both forces exist, both are real, but they act on you and the wall separately.

15Equal force, unequal motionExamples 6.7 and 6.8 · why the Earth doesn't move30 XP

The Earth pulls a falling fruit with the same force that the fruit pulls the Earth. Yet the fruit falls visibly while the Earth does not seem to move. Why?

Object A
—
Object B
—
Choose a scenario to compare the two accelerations.

Though the forces are equal in magnitude, the accelerations are not. By F = ma, the same force on a much larger mass produces a much smaller acceleration. The Earth's mass is so large (~6 × 10²⁴ kg) that its acceleration from a falling fruit is negligibly small.

A 0.1 kg bullet is fired from a 5 kg gun with a force of 2 N. What are the accelerations of the bullet and the gun?
Bullet: a = F/m = 2/0.1 = 20 m s⁻². Gun: a = F/m = 2/5 = 0.4 m s⁻². The gun recoils, but much less than the bullet moves. Same force, different masses, different accelerations — that is why you feel the recoil but the bullet flies far.

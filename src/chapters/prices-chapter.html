Market demand
—

Demand is the quantity of a product that people are willing and able to buy at a particular price, depending on their needs, preferences, season, trend and income. It is not just the desire to buy something — it is that desire complemented by the purchasing power to act on it.

The quantity one consumer wants at different prices, holding everything else constant, is individual demand; written out it is a demand schedule, and drawn it is a demand curve. The total quantity demanded by all potential buyers at each price is market demand — the sum of all the individual demands.

Don't miss out
The market demand curve is flatter than Srivalli's. Market demand aggregates many consumers, so the same price change creates a larger total quantity response. When the price falls from ₹150 to ₹50, Srivalli's demand rises by 2 kg — and market demand rises by 12 kg.
02Substitutes, or complements?Nine pairs of related goods30 XP

Related goods are products whose demand is interconnected: a change in the price or availability of one directly affects demand for the other. There are two kinds. Name each pair.

All nine rows must be answered before it will mark.

Substitute goods can replace each other — tea and coffee. If tea's price stays the same while coffee becomes more expensive, coffee drinkers may switch to tea, increasing tea's demand. When the price of a good rises, people replace it with a relatively cheaper alternative, so a rise in the price of the substitute raises demand for the other good.

Complementary goods are generally used together — smartphones and earphones, cars and petrol. If demand for printers rises, demand for cartridges may rise too, even though the cartridge price has not changed. And if movie tickets become more expensive, people may stay away from the cinema, so demand for the popcorn sold there falls with them.

Demand also depends on the size and composition of a nation's population. India, being the most populous nation, has domestic consumer demand that contributes to its economic growth — and the composition shapes what is demanded. More children means more demand for sports shoes; more working adults, for formal shoes; more elderly people, for comfortable or orthopaedic ones.

Why the fourth mango is disappointing
The first mango is delicious. The second is good. By the third or fourth you are barely interested. The additional utility derived from a product declines as more of it is consumed — the principle of diminishing marginal utility. And as the utility from each successive mango falls, so does your willingness to pay for it. Which is demand falling, from the inside.
04What demand actually meansWillingness, and the ability to act on it30 XP

Pick everything the chapter says — and leave the three it contradicts.

The Law of Demand is the inverse relationship between the price of a product and the quantity demanded of it. When the price rises the quantity demanded falls; when the price falls the quantity demanded rises. That is why a demand curve slopes downward — and it is a useful check on any table you are handed: if quantity demanded climbs as price climbs, something has been written down the wrong way round.

Purchasing power
A measure of how much one unit of a particular currency can buy at a particular time. It is the word that separates demand from wishing: a hundred people who want a mango and cannot pay for one are not demand for mangoes.

Market supply
—

Supply is the quantity of a product that sellers are willing and able to offer at a particular price. As the price increases, the quantity supplied increases; as it falls, the quantity supplied falls. That is the Law of Supply, and the reason is profit: higher prices increase profitability, which incentivises producers to raise output — and also attracts new firms into the market.

So a supply curve slopes upward, and a demand curve slopes downward, and everything in the second half of this chapter comes from those two facts pointing in opposite directions.

Why mangoes cost more in May
At the start of mango season supply is low, which makes mangoes costly. Mid-season the supply increases and prices fall. When supply is less than demand, prices rise; when supply exceeds demand, prices fall. The season has not changed anyone's taste for mangoes — it has changed how many there are.

Every market involves a negotiation between what buyers are willing to pay and what sellers are willing to accept, so prices are determined by the interaction of demand and supply. At ₹100 the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. That point is market equilibrium: there is no pressure for the price to change, and the market is cleared — neither a shortage (excess demand) nor a surplus (excess supply).

Real markets are dynamic. Changes in technology, wages and interest rates, and wars, political events, pandemics, weather and natural disasters all alter demand and supply. So equilibrium in the real world is never stable and moves all the time: the market is always in the process of adjusting to a new equilibrium, and never fully settles at the previous one.

Think about it
Can you think of another real-life case where prices change frequently, and say why? And a harder one the chapter asks outright: our choices today affect future resources — fast fashion, overfishing, the overuse of groundwater. Should we weigh only short-term gains, or long-term sustainability too? What would that do to the equilibrium?

Hotels may change the tariff several times in a day to earn maximum revenue — the total money a business earns from sales before any expenses are deducted. The changes depend on how fast rooms are getting booked, the tariff charged by nearby hotels, festivals, conferences or events in the area, weather forecasts, the number of days left before arrival, and past booking trends.

Not one of those six is the cost of cleaning the room. The price is not being worked out from what the room costs to provide; it is being worked out from what demand is doing, hour by hour.

A price ceiling is an imposed control setting the maximum a seller can charge — the government does this for essential goods like medicines, to prevent overcharging. A price floor is an imposed limit on how low a price can be charged; a minimum wage is one, set so that workers earn enough for their hard work. And the chapter adds a condition that is easy to miss: for a price floor to be effective, it must be set above the market equilibrium price.

The mirror of that condition is worth working out for yourself, and this bench will show it to you: a ceiling set above the equilibrium price changes nothing at all, because nobody was going to charge that much anyway.

Wheat at twenty rupees
If the government sets a maximum price for wheat at ₹20 per kg while market forces set it at ₹30, farmers receive less than they would in a free market. That can lead to reduced production and shortages — the same arithmetic this bench draws, happening to somebody's harvest.

14Both sides of interveningWhat it is for, and what it costs30 XP

Pick everything the chapter says about the government's role in a market — and leave the two it contradicts.

India is the fourth-largest economy in the world, and a market-based, regulated economy in which prices depend on demand and supply. But markets allocate goods on willingness and ability to pay, and that does not always come out fairly — which is why equity matters most for vulnerable and low-income groups, and why essential goods are exactly where governments intervene.

The chapter is just as clear about the other side. Fixing prices below market levels can sap producers' motivation to supply. Licences, permits and compliance procedures fall hardest on small enterprises and hamper the ease of doing business — a small restaurant may need food safety, fire safety, pollution control and local clearances before it opens. And heavy regulation reduces the incentive to invest in new ideas or better technology, which costs long-term productivity.

Sanitisers, 2020
Demand surged, there were stockouts and sharp price increases, and some shopkeepers began hoarding and black-marketing. The government declared sanitisers essential commodities under the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, capping the maximum retail price at ₹100 for a 200 ml bottle. Meanwhile many companies started production, and sanitisers soon became widely available at fair prices. The chapter then asks the fair question: that control was for an emergency — should such controls be in place forever?
